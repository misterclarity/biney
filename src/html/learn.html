What the research supports

Real but modest effects: decent evidence for anxiety and attention, weaker and noisier evidence for memory.

How a binaural beat is made

Feed 250 Hz to the left ear and 260 Hz to the right, and neither ear receives a 10 Hz signal — yet you perceive a 10 Hz pulsing. The beat is produced by binaural interaction in the superior olivary complex of the brainstem, which is why the effect vanishes the moment the tones reach both ears (Oster, 1973). Two constraints follow from the physiology:

  • Carrier matters. The lower of the two tones is the carrier, and lower carriers produce a more robust percept. A 40 Hz auditory steady-state response has been evoked with a 400 Hz carrier but was undetectable above roughly 3000 Hz.
  • The beat has a ceiling. Beyond about a 35 Hz difference, the two tones separate perceptually and the beat disappears — which is why gamma tracks sit at the edge of what this method can do.

Dosing: timing beats frequency-picking

The 2019 meta-analysis of 22 studies (35 effect sizes) found an overall medium effect, g = 0.45, and two practical findings that matter more than which exact frequency you choose:

  • Listen before the task, not only during it. Exposure before, or before and during, a task outperformed exposure during the task alone.
  • Longer is better. Time under exposure contributed significantly to the model. Most positive trials ran 15–30 minutes.
  • Masking is optional. White or pink noise over the top gave similar effects to unmasked beats — so the noise bed in the player is for comfort, not potency.

Where the evidence is strongest, and weakest

Anxiety is the best-supported outcome: theta/delta beats showed a medium-to-large effect, g = 0.69. Attention and vigilance hold up reasonably well. Memory is genuinely contested — a 2022 meta-analysis of 15 studies reported a medium overall effect, but the accompanying systematic review found conflicting results for theta and beta on memory tasks, and several 40 Hz studies found no entrainment at all with their parameters. Individual differences are real: alpha beats improved divergent thinking only in participants with lower estimated baseline dopamine.

Treat the strength bars on each card as what they are — a rough read of how consistent the literature is for that band, not a promise about your session.

The tinnitus track works differently

Everything else on this page rests on entrainment — a beat frequency nudging cortical rhythms. Notched Relief does not. Its active ingredient is the absence of sound: removing a one-octave band centred on your own tinnitus pitch is thought to reduce the over-excitable auditory cortex neurons tuned to that frequency, through lateral inhibition from their unmasked neighbours. That is why it needs a pitch match and why the notch is useless if it is aimed at the wrong frequency. The 8 Hz alpha beat rides on top because adding one to relaxing sound improved tinnitus handicap and emotional scores more than the sound alone.

Two caveats worth stating plainly. The notched-training results held for tinnitus at or below 8 kHz and were not observed above it. And the timescale is not one session: the trials ran 15 minutes twice daily for four weeks, or daily listening across twelve months for the roughly 30% loudness reduction. Sound therapy is an adjunct to clinical care, and for tinnitus distress specifically, cognitive behavioural therapy remains the better-supported intervention.

Breathing runs on a slower clock

The breathing guide works through a different route from the beats. Each breath nudges heart rate up on the way in and down on the way out. At around six breaths a minute, a rhythm of 0.1 Hz, that swing falls into step with the baroreflex, the loop that steadies blood pressure, and the two reinforce each other. Heart-rate variability peaks there, which is why HRV biofeedback trains people to breathe at that pace (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014). A 2022 meta-analysis of 223 studies found that voluntary slow breathing raised vagally mediated heart-rate variability during a session, straight after one, and after multi-session training.

The mood findings are more modest. A 2023 meta-analysis of randomised trials found breathwork lowered self-reported stress by a small-to-medium amount (g = −0.35), with similar effects on anxiety and depression, but most of the trials carried a moderate risk of bias. The trial behind cyclic sighing is randomised but small and was run remotely. As with the beats, read the strength bars as a summary of the literature, not a promise about your session.

With a heart-rate sensor connected, the guide shows how far your heart rate rises and falls within each breath. That peak-to-trough swing is one of the standard criteria for finding a person's resonance rate, which in adults usually lies between 4.5 and 6.5 breaths a minute. The resonance test follows the steps of the published assessment protocol, two minutes at each pace from 6.5 down to 4.5, but it skips the rest periods between steps and judges by the swing alone, without the phase and spectral criteria a clinician would also check (Shaffer & Meehan, 2020). Treat its answer as a good starting pace, not a diagnosis.

The coherence score follows HeartMath's published method. Every 5 seconds it takes the last 64 seconds of heartbeats, finds the strongest rhythm between 0.04 and 0.26 Hz, and compares the power in a 0.030 Hz band around that peak with the power below and above it: CR = (peak ÷ below) × (peak ÷ above), and the score is ln(CR + 1), usually between 0 and 8 (McCraty et al., 2009; Balaji et al., 2025). It measures how much your heart rhythm settles into one smooth wave, and slow, even breathing near your resonance rate is the most reliable way to raise it. In HeartMath's data from 1.8 million sessions, the most common peak was about 0.1 Hz, six breaths a minute, and sessions in which people reported positive emotions scored higher than those with negative ones. Feeling matters as well as breathing: in a 1995 laboratory study, holding a feeling of appreciation shifted heart-rate variability toward its mid- and high-frequency bands (McCraty et al., 1995). Heart Coherence follows HeartMath's Quick Coherence technique in that order: three minutes of slow, heart-focused breathing first, then gratitude, which is easiest to feel once the breathing has calmed you and the rhythm has settled, as it is after a meditation. HeartMath's own apps rescale the score (0 to 16 in Inner Balance) and use thresholds they don't publish, so expect the numbers here to differ from the app's. The score needs the time between heartbeats, so Entrain shows it only with a sensor that sends it, such as an ear-clip sensor or a chest strap. A watch averages its heart rate over a few seconds before sending it, which flattens the rise and fall the score looks for.

Guided practices and programs

The library adds guided sessions beyond paced breathing. They differ a lot in how well they're supported, so here is the evidence as it stands.

Mindfulness. Across 47 randomised trials with active controls, mindfulness meditation programs had moderate evidence for easing anxiety (effect size 0.38 at eight weeks), depression (0.30) and pain (0.33), and low evidence for stress; they didn't beat other active treatments such as exercise or therapy (Goyal et al., 2014). Anapana, the breath observation that opens a Vipassana course, belongs here: after the ten-day course, a community sample reported lower stress and higher well-being, partly kept at six months (Szekeres & Wertheim, 2015). The body scan on its own has weaker support. A meta-analysis of about 15 trials found a small gain in mindfulness (g = 0.27) and no clear effect on stress (Gan et al., 2022). A body-scan-based yoga nidra practice reduced stress slightly over two months, and its 30-minute version reduced sleep disturbance (Moszeik et al., 2025).

Kindness practices. Loving-kindness meditation had medium effects on daily positive emotions across 24 studies (Zeng et al., 2015). Self-compassion programs reduced rumination, stress, anxiety and depression across 27 randomised trials (Ferrari et al., 2019). The five-minute self-compassion break is their best-known single exercise, but it hasn't been tested on its own.

Gratitude. Across 27 trials with 3,675 people, gratitude exercises eased symptoms of depression and anxiety a little (g = 0.29), so for those symptoms better-tested treatments come first (Cregg & Cheavens, 2021); for everyday well-being, a meta-analysis found small gains (Davis et al., 2016). Writing down three good things each day for a week raised happiness and lowered depressive symptoms for six months in an online trial (Seligman et al., 2005). The Gratitude practice starts with a few minutes of slow breathing, because gratitude is easiest to feel once you're calm, then moves from something small to a person, your own body and the feeling itself, and ends with an invitation to note three good things.

Yoga and walking. In 42 randomised trials, practices that included yoga postures lowered evening and waking cortisol, ambulatory blood pressure and resting heart rate, and raised high-frequency heart-rate variability compared with active controls (Pascoe et al., 2017). Walking itself reduced depressive symptoms across 75 trials (Xu et al., 2024). The routine builder draws on a movement library of 56 poses and moves: 36 common asanas plus stretches, mobility drills and basic capoeira, each with its own drawing (animated for the moving ones), spoken cues and cautions. The evidence is for yoga practice in general rather than for any particular sequence, so build what suits your body and skip anything that hurts.

Pranayama. Most of this evidence comes from small physiological studies. Alternate nostril breathing lowered systolic blood pressure and raised a vagal heart-rate-variability measure in 26 men (Telles et al., 2014), though an earlier study found no clear change (Raghuraj et al., 1998). Humming raised nasal nitric oxide about 15-fold (Weitzberg & Lundberg, 2002), and raised heart-rate variability as much as slow breathing in a 16-person pilot (Woo & Kim, 2025). For beginners, slow breathing with equal in- and out-breaths improved baroreflex sensitivity most. Adding the ujjayi throat sound to both phases cancelled that gain, while ujjayi on the out-breath only kept it, which is how the Ujjayi practice here is set up (Mason et al., 2013). Kapalabhati works the other way, shifting heart-rate variability toward sympathetic activity (Raghuraj et al., 1998). It is an energiser with real contraindications, and fast breathwork is the least-studied corner of the field (Fincham et al., 2023).

Wim Hof breathing. Rounds of deep breathing followed by a hold on empty lungs are the best-studied fast breathing. In a controlled endotoxin study, men trained in the method (breathing, cold and meditation) released more adrenaline and had a weaker inflammatory response and fewer flu-like symptoms than untrained men (Kox et al., 2014). A follow-up found the breathing alone produced the anti-inflammatory effect, and cold added to it (Zwaag et al., 2022). A 2024 systematic review of eight small trials rated the risk of bias high and the evidence too thin to recommend the method yet (Almahayni & Hammond, 2024). The deep breathing lowers carbon dioxide, which delays the urge to breathe during the hold, and fainting is possible. In water that can be fatal, so the practice here is for sitting or lying down only, its hold ends by itself at three minutes, and it starts only after you confirm the cautions.

Relaxation and sleep. In a randomised comparison, progressive muscle relaxation increased felt relaxation and gave a steady physiological relaxation trend, while deep breathing briefly raised arousal first (Toussaint et al., 2021). Relaxation training is a conditionally recommended treatment for chronic insomnia (Edinger et al., 2021). Twenty minutes of slow breathing at six a minute before bed shortened the time to fall asleep and reduced night waking in people with self-reported insomnia (Tsai et al., 2015). The cognitive shuffle has only a conference study behind it so far (Beaudoin et al., 2016), and 5-4-3-2-1 grounding is a clinical tool with no direct trials.

Autogenic training. The Berlin psychiatrist Johannes Heinrich Schultz published autogenic training in 1932, and with Wolfgang Luthe made it a standard method: six exercises, each a short phrase said silently while you notice the body (heaviness, warmth, a calm heartbeat, the breath, a warm solar plexus and a cool forehead), learned one a week, practised two or three times a day, and ended with arms firm, a deep breath and eyes open (Schultz & Luthe, 1959). A meta-analysis of 60 controlled studies, 35 of them randomised, found medium-sized effects over control conditions, for tension headache and migraine, mild to moderate high blood pressure, anxiety disorders, mild to moderate depression and sleep problems among others, and results about as good as other psychological treatments, or a little smaller (Stetter & Kupper, 2002). A 2019 meta-analysis found it lowered anxiety in healthy adults and raised high-frequency heart-rate variability (Seo & Kim, 2019). At NASA's Ames Research Center, Patricia Cowings combined the exercises with biofeedback of heart rate, breathing, blood flow and skin conductance as Autogenic-Feedback Training, six hours of training that begins with paced breathing, against space motion sickness. On Spacelab 3 in 1985, the trained astronaut who learned to control his responses best had no severe symptom episodes, while the untrained crew members, who took medication, had several (Cowings et al., 1988); in a 2017 ground study, two hours of it before each test reduced the motion sickness of a simulated Orion re-entry (Cowings et al., 2017). Only a handful of astronauts have been studied. Entrain's version starts with paced breathing, as NASA's does, and a heart sensor gives the feedback.

Anxious or excited. Autogenic Reframing turns the method to anxious moments. Anxiety and excitement are nearly identical in the body: a faster heart, quicker breathing and a rush of adrenaline. In Brooks's experiments, heart rate rose before singing or a maths test whichever way people read it, and saying “I am excited” instead of trying to calm down made them feel more excited and do better (Brooks, 2014). Across randomised trials, reappraising stress arousal improved performance by a small amount (d = 0.22), more for public performances than written tests (Bosshard & Gomez, 2024). Nearly identical isn't quite the same: reading arousal as useful also shifted the cardiovascular response toward the more efficient pattern of a challenge rather than a threat (Jamieson et al., 2012). So the practice reframes the arousal rather than fighting it, while long out-breaths and the autogenic phrases take the edge off. It uses autogenic training's middle stage, where a personal resolution is said like one of the formulas, for “I am excited”, and rewrites the heart phrase to welcome a strong heartbeat as readiness. The combination hasn't been tested as a whole.

Why combine them? Each program pairs techniques whose evidence points at the same goal at the same moment. Sleep Descent joins a delta-bound beat with slow pre-sleep breathing. Calm Reset pairs alpha-to-theta beats with cyclic sighs and resonance breathing. Focus Primer puts a beta prime after brief heart-rate-variability breathing, which improved reaction times and accuracy on a stressful Stroop task (Prinsloo et al., 2011). Exercise Recovery joins theta beats with slow breathing after training (McConnell et al., 2014; Sugimoto et al., 2025). Anapana reminders can be added to any program: every few minutes, a quiet prompt brings attention back to the breath at the nostrils, the anchor of breath-awareness practice. No study has tested these combinations as packages, and a large 2023 trial found four weeks of coherent breathing didn't beat a placebo pace on self-reported stress (Fincham, Strauss & Cavanagh, 2023). A heart sensor is the honest check: each session ends with your own heart-rate change and coherence, so you can see what works for you.

Stretching and mobility. In a randomised trial with 134 workers at a logistics company, ten minutes of stretching after work for three months lowered anxiety compared with no programme, a moderate effect (partial η² = 0.06), and eased bodily pain and exhaustion (Montero-Marín et al., 2013). The Morning and Evening Stretch routines use the same kind of simple, everyday stretches, but haven't been tested themselves.

Capoeira. A 2025 systematic review found 19 studies of capoeira and health. Benefits for mental and social outcomes, such as attention, self-confidence, resilience and less aggression, were clearer than for physical health, where results for fitness, balance and bone density were inconclusive. Robust trials are still lacking, and most of the studies come from Portuguese-speaking countries (Köhler et al., 2025). Solo Capoeira teaches the basics on your own, to a synthesized berimbau playing the Angola rhythm.

Tapping. Emotional Freedom Techniques (EFT) pair tapping on acupressure points with attention on the problem and a self-acceptance statement. A review by the method's leading researchers counted 56 randomised trials, for conditions from anxiety and depression to pain and insomnia (Church et al., 2022). An independent 2025 review of seven trials in anxiety found reductions against no treatment and results similar to breathing or muscle relaxation, but couldn't pool the trials and judged most at some risk of bias (Choi et al., 2025). Whether the tapping itself matters is the weak point: in the three studies that could be pooled, real points did better than sham or no tapping by a moderate, borderline-significant margin (d = −0.47, 95% CI −0.94 to 0.0; Church et al., 2018). The nine points here are the standard ones: the side of the hand, then the top of the head, eyebrow, side of the eye, under the eye, under the nose, chin, collarbone and under the arm.

Naps. After a short night, a 10-minute afternoon nap improved alertness and performance straight away, with some gains lasting about two and a half hours. A 20-minute nap helped from about half an hour after waking, and a 30-minute nap brought a spell of grogginess first (Brooks & Lack, 2006). Heart rate drops at each transition from waking into light sleep and rises again with brief arousals (Burgess et al., 1999), which is what the Power Nap's sleep estimate relies on. It can't see sleep stages, so treat it as a guide.

Heart-led beats. This program is an experiment that turns your heart data into sound, the idea behind biofeedback: as your heart rate settles below your baseline and its variability and coherence rise, the binaural beat glides from alpha toward theta. No study has tested binaural beats driven by the heart, and the rule is Entrain's own, so the chart at the end, your heart rate with the beat drawn over it, is the honest test.

Nonviolent communication. Marshall Rosenberg's method separates four things that usually blur together: what happened, what you feel, what you need, and what you'd like to ask for (Rosenberg, 2015). The two Reflection practices turn it inward. Judgments, labels and “shoulds” are read as signs of needs that aren't being met, and as rules you may have absorbed without choosing them, so questioning one becomes a small, personal kind of ideology critique. Research on NVC itself is early: in a randomised study with 312 medical students, two and a half days of training raised self-rated empathy three months later, though not other measures of empathy (Epinat-Duclos et al., 2021), and a 2025 review of 15 mostly uncontrolled studies found gains in empathy alongside weak methods (Pereira & Durgante, 2025). The parts the practices lean on are better studied. Putting feelings into words dampens their intensity (Torre & Lieberman, 2018); people who tell their emotions apart more precisely are less likely to cope in harmful ways when distressed (Kashdan et al., 2015), which is what the feelings wheel is for: 88 words in 11 families, to get from “bad” to “discouraged” or “resentful”; writing about difficult experiences has a small but reliable benefit across 146 randomised studies (Frattaroli, 2006); and in self-determination theory, acting from your own values goes with more well-being than acting from pressure, guilt or the need for approval (Ryan & Deci, 2000). What you write stays on your device, and you can keep nothing at all. The closing line paraphrases Carl Rogers's observation in On Becoming a Person (1961) that accepting yourself as you are is what makes change possible.

Difficult conversations. Four practices cover a hard conversation or a stressful meeting: Prepare a Hard Conversation (NVC) to get clear first, Difficult Conversation to get your body ready, During the Call beside a video call, and After the Conversation. The preparation asks what makes the conversation hard for you, including what it might say about you if it went badly, which the Harvard Negotiation Project calls the identity conversation (Stone, Patton & Heen, 1999), then your feelings and needs, theirs, a request and a first sentence. Writing about your worries for ten minutes just before an exam improved anxious students' results (Ramirez & Beilock, 2011). Getting ready starts in the body. Slow breathing at your resonance pace is the core of heart-rate-variability biofeedback, which reduced self-reported stress and anxiety with a large effect across 24 studies (g = 0.83; Goessl et al., 2017). The out-breath is the longer part here because at six breaths a minute a longer exhale felt more relaxing (Van Diest et al., 2014), though it didn't raise heart-rate variability more than even breaths did (Meehan & Shaffer, 2024). The beat plays before the conversation, where the meta-analysis found clearer effects than with listening only during a task; it settles into theta, the band with the larger effects on anxiety, then climbs back to alpha so you go in awake (Garcia-Argibay et al., 2019); that shape is Entrain's own. Four short steps come from experiments on performing under social pressure. Saying “I'm excited” rather than trying to calm down made speeches more persuasive and singing and maths more accurate (Brooks, 2014), and reading a racing heart as your body getting ready gave a more efficient cardiovascular response during a speech task (Jamieson et al., 2012). Reflecting on a value of your own beforehand blunted the cortisol rise to a stressful speech (Creswell et al., 2005). If-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals across 94 tests (d = 0.65; Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006), and talking to yourself by name or as “you”, as the plan is worded here, led to better speeches with less distress and less brooding afterwards (Kross et al., 2014). During the call, slow breathing at a moment of anxiety in daily life lowered it from just before to just after, though across weeks the effect on anxiety was mixed (Lachowicz et al., 2026); the beat plays at half volume on a low carrier so voices stay clear. Afterwards, a brief self-compassion exercise right after a speech reduced socially anxious students' brooding about it the next day (Blackie & Kocovski, 2018), and naming the feeling dampens it (Torre & Lieberman, 2018). None of the four has been tested as a whole.

Background sound and quotes. Summer rain and crickets are synthesized on your device, like the pink-noise bed, and are there for comfort: masking sounds didn't weaken binaural beat effects in the research above. The quotes come from public-domain translations (George Long's Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus, Richard Gummere's Seneca, Max Müller's Dhammapada and James Legge's Tao Te Ching) and Thoreau's Walden. Carl Rogers's line is a paraphrase, since his book is still in copyright, and the lines signed Entrain are its own.

Safety

Entrain is for everyday stress, sleep and focus. It isn't a medical device or a treatment: nothing here diagnoses, treats or prevents any condition, and its heart readings are for practice, not for checking your health. Talk to a clinician about problems with sleep, anxiety or attention.

  • The beats. Ask your doctor first if you have epilepsy or another seizure disorder: the beats are meant to nudge the brain's rhythms, and their safety for people with epilepsy hasn't been established. Never listen while driving, cycling or operating machinery, since the slower beats are made to relax you or send you to sleep. Keep the volume low.
  • Headphones with a pacemaker. The magnets in headphones and earbuds can interfere with a pacemaker or an implanted defibrillator. Keep them at least 15 cm (6 in) from it: not around your neck or in a breast pocket (American Heart Association).
  • Breathing. Breathe gently, and stop to breathe normally if you feel light-headed, dizzy or tingly. Leave out the breath holds and the fast breathing (Wim Hof, Kapalabhati) if you are pregnant or have a heart, lung or blood-pressure condition or epilepsy, and never do the fast breathing in or near water, standing up or while driving.
  • Moving. Go only as far as is comfortable, stop anything that hurts, and keep clear space around you.
  • Feelings. A body scan, tapping or a reflection can bring up strong feelings. If one does, stop, look around the room and breathe slowly, and talk to someone you trust or a professional if it keeps happening.
  • Food and supplements. Supplements aren't checked like medicines, and some interact with them: ask a doctor or pharmacist before you start one if you take medicines (blood thinners above all), are pregnant or breastfeeding, have kidney or liver disease, or have an operation coming up. The amounts in Healthy living are those used in studies, not prescriptions.

Check with a clinician before you start if you have a heart, lung or seizure condition, are pregnant, or are in treatment for anxiety, trauma or depression. Practices with cautions of their own give them in their introduction.
