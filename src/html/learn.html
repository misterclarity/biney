Brain-wave states

Brain waves are the rhythmic electrical activity of large groups of neurons, measured with EEG and named by how many cycles they complete each second (Hz). At any moment the brain makes a mix of all of them: what changes with your state, and with your age, is which one leads.

Theta has two faces

Theta covers roughly 4 to 8 Hz, and in adults it turns up in two quite different situations. One is drowsy or inward: falling asleep, light sleep, deep meditation, a drifting, dreamy awareness. The other is focused mental work: theta over the front of the brain increases during concentration, working memory and learning. So theta isn't simply a “dreamy” or “hypnotic” state. What it means depends on where in the brain it appears and on what the person is doing.

A typical day

For a healthy adult of around 40, the lead changes hands through the day:

  • Working, talking, planning: beta leads, with gamma during bursts of intense focus.
  • Relaxing on the sofa, eyes closed, a short break: alpha takes over. Most adults' resting rhythm sits near 10 Hz, and closing your eyes is the quickest way to raise it: cutting off what you see lifts alpha within seconds, one of the most reliable effects known in EEG.
  • Falling asleep: alpha fades into theta.
  • Deep sleep: delta leads.
  • Dreaming (REM sleep): a mixed, faster pattern that looks a lot like waking activity.

How children's rhythms develop

Children's brain activity is slower than adults' and speeds up steadily as they grow.

  • Infancy. Slow delta activity leads at first. The main rhythm, about 5 Hz (theta) at five months, quickens to 6 or 7 Hz by the end of the first year and to 7–9 Hz in the third (Eisermann et al., 2013). In 592 children followed from 2 to 44 months of age, the brain's rhythms appeared and shifted in steps that the authors read as milestones of brain maturation (Wilkinson et al., 2024).
  • Ages 3 to 7. A 2021 study of 96 people aged 3 to 24 found early childhood marked by theta leading at the back of the head, with the main rhythm shifting from theta to alpha around the age of 7 or 8 (Cellier et al., 2021).
  • The clinical view. Guides to children's EEG put the main resting rhythm in the alpha range, from about 8 Hz, by about the age of 3 (Eisermann et al., 2013). The difference comes partly from how it's measured and where the bands are drawn: the 2021 study separated true rhythms from the brain's broadband background activity, which also changes with age.
  • Into adulthood. The alpha peak keeps speeding up through adolescence into the early twenties (Cellier et al., 2021).

In short, “children under seven are mostly in theta” is a reasonable simplification. The popular follow-up claim, that this puts young children in a hypnotic, “downloading” state that programmes their subconscious, comes from self-help writing rather than neuroscience research.

Reading brain waves with care

  • Linked, not caused. “Best for” describes the activities each band is linked with. It doesn't mean that producing more of a band makes you better at those activities.
  • A result, not a switch. Brain waves are a result of what the brain is doing, not a switch you can flip. Claims that apps, binaural beats or audio tracks reliably “put you in theta” have mixed or weak evidence, and that includes Entrain's beats: the research on them is set out below.
  • Averages. EEG findings come from averages across groups, and individuals vary a lot.

Measuring at home. Consumer EEG headbands such as the Muse do best at sleep. In a 2025 study of 56 adults, the Muse S matched a full laboratory sleep study with 88–96% accuracy across the sleep stages, though it counted about 15 minutes too much deep sleep and about as much too little light sleep (Lanthier et al., 2025). Reading the brain waves themselves is harder: compared with research-grade EEG, the Muse matched worst of the three consumer devices tested (Mikhaylov et al., 2024). It has only four dry sensors, on the forehead and behind the ears, so blinks, a tense jaw and movement all show up in its signal. That makes it good enough for broad, relative feedback, such as “more relaxed than a minute ago” or “alpha is rising”, but not for “you are in theta now”: a smoothed, averaged trend says more than any one reading.

What the research supports

Real but modest effects: decent evidence for anxiety and attention, weaker and noisier evidence for memory.

How a binaural beat is made

Feed 250 Hz to the left ear and 260 Hz to the right, and neither ear receives a 10 Hz signal — yet you perceive a 10 Hz pulsing. The beat is produced by binaural interaction in the superior olivary complex of the brainstem, which is why the effect vanishes the moment the tones reach both ears (Oster, 1973). Two constraints follow from the physiology:

  • Carrier matters. The lower of the two tones is the carrier, and lower carriers produce a more robust percept. A 40 Hz auditory steady-state response has been evoked with a 400 Hz carrier but was undetectable above roughly 3000 Hz.
  • The beat has a ceiling. Beyond about a 35 Hz difference, the two tones separate perceptually and the beat disappears — which is why gamma tracks sit at the edge of what this method can do.

Monaural beats and isochronic tones

Two other ways of making a beat need no headphones. A monaural beat mixes the two tones before they reach you: 250 Hz and 260 Hz together in both ears, so the sound itself swells and fades ten times a second, in the air as well as in the ear (Oster, 1973). An isochronic tone is a single tone switched on and off at the beat; Entrain rounds the edges of each pulse so it doesn't click. Both follow the same beat as the binaural version: the same carrier, the same slide from one frequency to another, the same changes while a practice runs, and the beat that follows your heart.

The guide to brain-wave states rates them like binaural beats: the evidence is mixed, and similar. A review of beat stimulation, binaural and monaural, found its results often contradictory (Chaieb et al., 2015), and most of the research on this page tested binaural beats, so it carries over to the other two only roughly. Choose by what you have: binaural with headphones, monaural or isochronic on speakers. A phone's own small speaker plays low tones quietly, so a bigger speaker, or earphones, carries them better. The Sound choice is on every track's page, the beat builder, the custom primer and a practice's Options, with the default in Settings; the speaker ways are among the new techniques that open with a donation. The tinnitus track stays binaural, as its studies played it.

Dosing: timing beats frequency-picking

The 2019 meta-analysis of 22 studies (35 effect sizes) found an overall medium effect, g = 0.45 (Garcia-Argibay et al., 2019), and three practical findings that matter more than which exact frequency you choose:

  • Listen before the task, not only during it. Exposure before, or before and during, a task outperformed exposure during the task alone.
  • Longer is better. Time under exposure contributed significantly to the model. Most positive trials ran 15–30 minutes.
  • Masking is optional. White or pink noise over the top gave similar effects to unmasked beats — so the noise bed in the player is for comfort, not potency.

Where the evidence is strongest, and weakest

Anxiety is the best-supported outcome: theta/delta beats showed a medium-to-large effect, g = 0.69 (Garcia-Argibay et al., 2019). Attention and vigilance hold up reasonably well. Memory is genuinely contested — a meta-analysis of 15 studies found a medium overall effect on memory and attention, but its systematic review found conflicting results, especially for theta and beta (Basu & Banerjee, 2023), and a 40 Hz trial found no change in memory scores or EEG, concluding the beats never entrained the brain (Borges et al., 2023). Individual differences are real: alpha beats improved divergent thinking only in participants with lower estimated baseline dopamine (Reedijk et al., 2013).

The tinnitus track works differently

Everything else on this page rests on entrainment — a beat frequency nudging cortical rhythms. Notched Relief does not. Its active ingredient is the absence of sound: removing a one-octave band centred on your own tinnitus pitch is thought to reduce the over-excitable auditory cortex neurons tuned to that frequency, through lateral inhibition from their unmasked neighbours (Okamoto et al., 2010). That is why it needs a pitch match and why the notch is useless if it is aimed at the wrong frequency. The 8 Hz alpha beat on top has weaker support. Added to relaxing music, it didn't lower tinnitus handicap more than the music alone after four weeks, though two subscales favoured it three months later (Bakhtarikia et al., 2024); added to notched music, it gave a larger but not significant drop in annoyance (Samaei et al., 2026).

Two caveats worth stating plainly. The notched-training results held for tinnitus at or below 8 kHz and were not observed above it (Teismann et al., 2011). And the timescale is not one session: the trials with the beat ran 15 or 20 minutes twice daily for four weeks, and the roughly 30% loudness reduction came after twelve months of daily listening. Sound therapy is an adjunct to clinical care, and for tinnitus distress specifically, cognitive behavioural therapy remains the better-supported intervention.

Breathing runs on a slower clock

The breathing guide works through a different route from the beats. Each breath nudges heart rate up on the way in and down on the way out. At around six breaths a minute, a rhythm of 0.1 Hz, that swing falls into step with the baroreflex, the loop that steadies blood pressure, and the two reinforce each other. Heart-rate variability peaks there, which is why HRV biofeedback trains people to breathe at that pace (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014). A 2022 meta-analysis of 223 studies found that voluntary slow breathing raised vagally mediated heart-rate variability during a session, straight after one, and after multi-session training.

The mood findings are more modest. A 2023 meta-analysis of randomised trials found breathwork lowered self-reported stress by a small-to-medium amount (g = −0.35), with similar effects on anxiety and depression, but most of the trials carried a moderate risk of bias. The trial behind cyclic sighing is randomised but small and was run remotely. As with the beats, read the strength bars as a summary of the literature, not a promise about your session.

With a heart-rate sensor connected, the guide shows how far your heart rate rises and falls within each breath. That peak-to-trough swing is one of the standard criteria for finding a person's resonance rate, which in adults usually lies between 4.5 and 6.5 breaths a minute. The resonance test follows the steps of the published assessment protocol, two minutes at each pace from 6.5 down to 4.5, but it skips the rest periods between steps and judges by the swing alone, without the phase and spectral criteria a clinician would also check (Shaffer & Meehan, 2020). Treat its answer as a good starting pace, not a diagnosis.

The coherence score follows HeartMath's published method. Every 5 seconds it takes the last 64 seconds of heartbeats, finds the strongest rhythm between 0.04 and 0.26 Hz, and compares the power in a 0.030 Hz band around that peak with the power below and above it: CR = (peak ÷ below) × (peak ÷ above), and the score is ln(CR + 1), usually between 0 and 8 (McCraty et al., 2009; Balaji et al., 2025). It measures how much your heart rhythm settles into one smooth wave, and slow, even breathing near your resonance rate is the most reliable way to raise it. In HeartMath's data from 1.8 million sessions, the most common peak was about 0.1 Hz, six breaths a minute, and sessions in which people reported positive emotions scored higher than those with negative ones. Feeling matters as well as breathing: in a 1995 laboratory study, holding a feeling of appreciation shifted heart-rate variability towards its mid- and high-frequency bands (McCraty et al., 1995). Heart Coherence follows HeartMath's Quick Coherence technique in that order: three minutes of slow, heart-focused breathing first, then gratitude, which is easiest to feel once the breathing has calmed you and the rhythm has settled, as it is after a meditation. HeartMath's own apps rescale the score (0 to 16 in Inner Balance) and use thresholds they don't publish, so expect the numbers here to differ from the app's. The score needs the time between heartbeats, so Entrain shows it only with a sensor that sends it, such as an ear-clip sensor or a chest strap. A watch averages its heart rate over a few seconds before sending it, which flattens the rise and fall the score looks for.

The Heart page explains each of its numbers and charts under them. RMSSD, the change from one heartbeat to the next, is the main short-term measure of the variability the vagus nerve puts into the heartbeat, and breathing slower than about 8.5 breaths a minute moves the breathing rhythm into the spectrum's low-frequency band, 0.04 to 0.15 Hz, where the blood-pressure reflex also works (Shaffer & Ginsberg, 2017). Breath from the microphone, an option there, listens for your breathing: it keeps the sound between 0.5 and 5 kHz, where breath noise lies, measures its loudness ten times a second and finds the rhythm in it, much as a study did with a headset microphone under the nose, which read most breathing rates to within about 1% but couldn't read 6 breaths a minute reliably, the slow rhythm lost in background noise (Nam et al., 2016). Entrain listens over a full minute, to give slow breathing more breaths to go on, and says how sure it is. It listens through the phone's own microphone, because Bluetooth earbuds switch to a mono call mode while their own microphone is in use, which would undo a binaural beat. The coherence score doesn't use it and stays HeartMath's, from the heartbeat alone, so it stays comparable with their published figures. What the microphone adds is your actual breathing pace, the dashed line on the spectrum, so you can see whether the rhythm the score finds is your breathing, and the swing of each breath without a breathing session. Nothing is recorded or sent: the sound becomes one loudness number every tenth of a second on your device, and only the last two minutes are kept, for the chart.

The Stress Check, under Calm, asks how stressed you feel and reads your heart at rest for two or five minutes. Stress lowers heart-rate variability, above all the vagal, beat-to-beat part that RMSSD measures (Kim et al., 2018), and two minutes are enough for RMSSD: in 3,387 adults, two-minute stretches agreed closely with five-minute recordings, and more closely than SDNN did (Muñoz et al., 2015). RMSSD differs so much between people, and falls so steadily with age, that Entrain doesn't set yours against anyone else's; published norms also differ with sex, posture and how they were measured (Shaffer & Ginsberg, 2017). Your first three checks set a baseline instead, and from the fourth each one is read against the middle of your last ten. The cut-offs that turn the two readings, your heart's and your own, into a level are Entrain's own and haven't been tested, and a check within half an hour of another session shows what that session changed rather than joining the baseline. The advice puts the two best-supported tools first: slow breathing at your resonance rate, which raises heart-rate variability during and after a session (Laborde et al., 2022) and, as biofeedback, lowered self-reported stress and anxiety (Goessl et al., 2017), though four weeks of coherent breathing didn't beat a placebo pace on stress (Fincham et al., 2023); and cyclic sighing, which lifted mood and slowed resting breathing the most in a month-long trial (Balban et al., 2023). Its beats follow the binaural-beat evidence, where theta and delta beats eased anxiety the most (Garcia-Argibay et al., 2019).

Breath training for swimming and diving

Breathing capacity has three parts, and they train very differently: how big your lungs are, how strong the muscles that fill them are, and how long you can go without a breath. Lung size changes least. Elite swimmers do have big lungs, with total lung capacity at 128% and vital capacity at 124% of the values predicted for adult men (Armour et al., 1993), but how they got them is unsettled. Girls aged 11 to 14 who swam competitively already had larger lungs than their peers before a season began, and a seven-month season didn't widen the gap (Bovard et al., 2018); a review concluded that years of training while the lungs grow may explain it, but that genetics and selection into the sport can't be ruled out (Rochat et al., 2022). Training the breathing muscles doesn't enlarge the lungs either: across 13 studies of swimmers, inspiratory muscle training raised inspiratory strength while forced vital capacity and FEV1 stayed the same (Carvajal-Tello et al., 2024).

The breathing muscles respond well, and hard swimming tires them: after a 200 m front-crawl swim at 90% of race pace, inspiratory strength had fallen by 21% when swimmers breathed every fourth stroke and by 11% when they breathed every second (Jakovljevic & McConnell, 2009). In club swimmers, six weeks of inspiratory muscle training, 30 breaths twice a day through a handheld spring-loaded valve set at half their maximum, cut 100 m times by 1.7% and 200 m times by 1.5% against a sham device, with no gain at 400 m (Kilding et al., 2010). Pooled across ten trials, respiratory muscle training improved swimming performance by a small to moderate amount (Liu et al., 2025), but an analysis of 14 trials in 375 competitive swimmers found stronger breathing muscles and better FEV1 with no significant change in 50, 100 or 200 m times (Chen et al., 2026). This kind of training needs the device; an app can only count the breaths.

Breath-hold time is the most trainable part, and the Swim & dive pattern on Breathe paces this kind of dry training. Within one session, a series of maximal holds can stretch, helped, it seems, by the spleen squeezing stored red blood cells into the circulation: over five holds two minutes apart, with the face in cold water, haematocrit rose 6.4% in people with a spleen, and only in them did the urge to breathe come later as the series went on (Schagatay et al., 2001). With training, holds lengthen mainly because the first involuntary breathing movements, the body's push to breathe, come later, a point set mainly by carbon dioxide, and because the diving response, the reflex slowing of the heart that saves oxygen, grows stronger (Schagatay et al., 2000). In ten untrained people, two weeks of ten maximal holds a day lengthened the longest hold by 44 seconds; the diving response began 3 seconds sooner, and the longest holds took oxygen saturation down to 72%, from 84% before training (Engan et al., 2013). How far this carries into sport is less clear: across seven studies, breath-hold training raised peak blood lactate but gave no evidence of an effect on aerobic markers such as VO2max (de Asís-Fernández et al., 2022).

In the pool, the methods with the clearest results limit breathing during hard swimming, under a coach. Triathletes who twice a week swam a set of 12 to 20 × 25 m sprints with short holds after breathing out, at low lung volume, improved their 100, 200 and 400 m times by 3.5 to 4.4% in five weeks, while those who swam the same set breathing normally didn't change (Woorons et al., 2016). In a trial with 16 competitive swimmers, six sessions of repeated sprints with the same method raised the number of all-out 25 m sprints they could repeat from 7.1 to 9.6, while those who breathed normally didn't improve significantly (Trincat et al., 2017). Breathing only every 7 to 10 strokes kept college swimmers' breathing muscles from tiring in a race but didn't make them faster (Burtch et al., 2017).

What makes a hold longer is also what makes it dangerous. Breathing fast and deep beforehand blows off carbon dioxide, which delays the urge to breathe while oxygen keeps falling, so a swimmer can black out underwater without warning. A review of drownings and near-drownings in New York State found 16 people harmed by dangerous breath-holding; the four who died were advanced or expert swimmers aged 17 to 22 who had hyperventilated first (Boyd et al., 2015). In a laboratory, 15 seconds of hyperventilation before each of five holds made them longer, 133 against 111 seconds, but took oxygen saturation lower, and lower with each hold, from 94.0% in the first to 86.7% in the last, which the authors warn could heighten the risk of a blackout (Pernett et al., 2023). Lung packing, gulping extra air on top of a full breath, impairs the return of blood to the heart and can make you faint (Schipke et al., 2019). So Swim & dive is for dry land only, sitting or lying down, with normal breathing before each hold, and it's done entirely at your own risk. In the water, breath holds belong under a coach or a trained buddy watching the whole time.

Guided practices and programmes

The library adds guided sessions beyond paced breathing. They differ a lot in how well they're supported, so here is the evidence as it stands.

Mindfulness. Across 47 randomised trials with active controls, mindfulness meditation programmes had moderate evidence for easing anxiety (effect size 0.38 at eight weeks), depression (0.30) and pain (0.33), and low evidence for stress; they didn't beat other active treatments such as exercise or therapy (Goyal et al., 2014). Anapana, the breath observation that opens a Vipassana course, belongs here: after the ten-day course, a community sample reported lower stress and higher well-being, partly kept at six months (Szekeres & Wertheim, 2015). The body scan on its own has weaker support. A meta-analysis of about 15 trials found a small gain in mindfulness (g = 0.27) and no clear effect on stress (Gan et al., 2022). A body-scan-based yoga nidra practice reduced stress slightly over two months, and its 30-minute version reduced sleep disturbance (Moszeik et al., 2025).

Kindness practices. Loving-kindness meditation had medium effects on daily positive emotions across 24 studies (Zeng et al., 2015). Self-compassion programmes reduced rumination, stress, anxiety and depression across 27 randomised trials (Ferrari et al., 2019). The five-minute self-compassion break is their best-known single exercise, but it hasn't been tested on its own.

Gratitude. Across 27 trials with 3,675 people, gratitude exercises eased symptoms of depression and anxiety a little (g = 0.29), so for those symptoms better-tested treatments come first (Cregg & Cheavens, 2021); for everyday well-being, a meta-analysis found small gains (Davis et al., 2016). Writing down three good things each day for a week raised happiness and lowered depressive symptoms for six months in an online trial (Seligman et al., 2005). The Gratitude practice starts with a few minutes of slow breathing, because gratitude is easiest to feel once you're calm, then moves from something small to a person, your own body and the feeling itself, and ends with an invitation to note three good things.

Mindful eating. How you eat changes how much you eat, and how much you notice of it. Across 24 studies, eating while distracted, by a screen for instance, made people eat more at that meal and more again later, while remembering a meal well reduced how much they ate later (Robinson et al., 2013). Eating more slowly lowered how much people ate without leaving them hungrier at the end of the meal (Robinson et al., 2014), and chewing longer lowered hunger (Miquel-Kergoat et al., 2015). Mindfulness meditation reduced binge eating and emotional eating, though its effect on weight was mixed (Katterman et al., 2014), and teenagers who wrote a letter of gratitude each week for a month reported healthier eating than those who listed their activities (Fritz et al., 2019). The Mindful Meal puts these together, with a few slow breaths first and an optional binaural beat; the combination, and a beat at a meal, haven't been tested. It's about noticing and enjoying a meal rather than eating less. An easy walk afterwards suits it: short bouts of light walking lowered the rise in blood sugar and insulin after meals, compared with sitting still (Buffey et al., 2022).

Waking up. The grogginess after waking, sleep inertia, usually fades within 15 to 30 minutes, though full alertness can take an hour, and a 2019 review found no wake-up routine with clear evidence of speeding it up: sound looked promising, light made people feel more alert without improving their performance, and exercise hadn't been tested (Hilditch & McHill, 2019). Morning daylight is the body clock's strongest signal that the day has begun (Brown et al., 2022). Fast yoga breathing shifts the heart rhythm towards arousal, the opposite of slow breathing (Raghuraj et al., 1998), and a session of it improved attention in 11- and 12-year-olds (Telles et al., 2019). Beta binaural beats improved vigilance compared with theta and delta beats (Lane et al., 1998). Morning Wake-Up puts these together as Sleep Descent the other way round: a stretch on waking, breathing that quickens with the in-breath leading, a beat rising from 6 Hz theta to 16 Hz beta, stretches and standing poses, then daylight. The combination hasn't been tested.

Sport. What you say to yourself matters: two weeks of practising motivational self-talk lengthened cycling time to exhaustion by about 18% and made the effort feel easier (Blanchfield et al., 2014), and across 32 studies self-talk improved performance, motivational words most for strength and endurance (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011). Rehearsing in the mind's eye improved sports performance with a medium effect (d = 0.43) across studies (Simonsmeier et al., 2021). Runners kept going longer with a metronome or music matched to their cadence than in silence (Bood et al., 2013), and music in general helps a little, lifting mood and output and easing the effort felt (Terry et al., 2020). Psyching up before lifting may raise strength, though the evidence is tentative (Tod et al., 2003). For rowers, a breathing-muscle warm-up with a resistance trainer added 1.2% to a six-minute all-out effort (Volianitis et al., 2001). Holding the breath to brace for a heavy lift raises the pressure in the belly, which steadies the spine, and blood pressure with it, so it's a skill to learn with light loads (Hackett & Chow, 2013). Binaural beats in sport are barely studied: 15 Hz beta beats with music in the rests between rounds helped kickboxers strike faster, in a trial that couldn't be blinded (Jebabli et al., 2025), while a 10 Hz beat with music changed nothing measurable for recreational runners (Park et al., 2026). Sports Performance leans on the better-supported parts, your words, your imagery and a cadence, with the beat in the background.

Creative thinking. Ideas come more freely in a relaxed, wide-open state. Open-monitoring meditation, letting thoughts come and go, raised the number and variety of ideas in experienced meditators, where focused attention didn't (Colzato et al., 2012). Walking, on a treadmill or outdoors, raised creative output in four experiments, and the boost lingered when people sat down again (Oppezzo & Schwartz, 2014). Positive, energised moods help creative thinking (Baas et al., 2008). At least 15 seconds in the lightest stage of sleep tripled the chance of finding a hidden shortcut in a maths task (Lacaux et al., 2021): the edge of sleep Edison is said to have caught by dozing with something in his hand. Alpha brain activity rises when people come up with ideas (Fink & Benedek, 2014), and stimulating the front of the brain at 10 Hz raised creativity scores by about 7% (Lustenberger et al., 2015). Binaural beats are a weaker lever: alpha and gamma beats helped idea generation only in people whose blink rate pointed to lower dopamine (Reedijk et al., 2013). Creative Flow puts the open awareness, the play, the walk and the twilight together, under an alpha beat that dips to theta while ideas brew; the sequence itself hasn't been tested.

Mindful sex. Mindfulness is one of the better-tested tools of sex therapy: four group sessions raised sexual desire, arousal and satisfaction and lowered sexual distress in 117 women with low desire (Brotto & Basson, 2014), and across seven trials mindfulness therapies improved sexual function moderately (d = 0.55; Banbury et al., 2023). Sensate focus, touch in turns with the attention on sensation rather than on performance, remains a core technique (Avery-Clark et al., 2019). Arousal has a sweet spot: women's genital arousal was highest with moderate activation of the sympathetic nervous system, and lower when it was very low or very high (Lorenz et al., 2012), and a single session of autogenic relaxation raised both physical and felt arousal (Stanton & Meston, 2017). Theta for slow lovemaking and alpha for intensity? No study has tested binaural beats during sex. Theta is the band of deep relaxation and meditative absorption, and alpha of relaxed wakefulness, so each suits one of the two moods; but the few EEG studies of arousal found faster, beta and gamma, activity rising with it (Guevara et al., 2018), and one theory holds that rhythm itself carries arousal towards a trance-like peak (Safron, 2016). In Mindful Sex the beat sets the mood; the breathing, the touch and the attention do the work.

Yoga and walking. In 42 randomised trials, practices that included yoga postures lowered evening and waking cortisol, ambulatory blood pressure and resting heart rate, and raised high-frequency heart-rate variability compared with active controls (Pascoe et al., 2017). Walking itself reduced depressive symptoms across 75 trials (Xu et al., 2024). The routine builder draws on a movement library of 56 poses and moves: 36 common asanas plus stretches, mobility drills and basic capoeira, each with its own drawing (animated for the moving ones), spoken cues and cautions. The evidence is for yoga practice in general rather than for any particular sequence, so build what suits your body and skip anything that hurts.

Pranayama. Most of this evidence comes from small physiological studies. Alternate nostril breathing lowered systolic blood pressure and raised a vagal heart-rate-variability measure in 26 men (Telles et al., 2014), though an earlier study found no clear change (Raghuraj et al., 1998). Humming raised nasal nitric oxide about 15-fold (Weitzberg & Lundberg, 2002), and raised heart-rate variability as much as slow breathing in a 16-person pilot (Woo & Kim, 2025). For beginners, slow breathing with equal in- and out-breaths improved baroreflex sensitivity most. Adding the ujjayi throat sound to both phases cancelled that gain, while ujjayi on the out-breath only kept it, which is how the Ujjayi practice here is set up (Mason et al., 2013). Kapalabhati works the other way, shifting heart-rate variability towards sympathetic activity (Raghuraj et al., 1998). It is an energiser with real contraindications, and fast breathwork is the least-studied corner of the field (Fincham et al., 2023).

Wim Hof breathing. Rounds of deep breathing followed by a hold on empty lungs are the best-studied fast breathing. In a controlled endotoxin study, men trained in the method (breathing, cold and meditation) released more adrenaline and had a weaker inflammatory response and fewer flu-like symptoms than untrained men (Kox et al., 2014). A follow-up found the breathing alone produced the anti-inflammatory effect, and cold added to it (Zwaag et al., 2022). A 2024 systematic review of eight small trials rated the risk of bias high and the evidence too thin to recommend the method yet (Almahayni & Hammond, 2024). The deep breathing lowers carbon dioxide, which delays the urge to breathe during the hold, and fainting is possible. In water that can be fatal, so the practice here is for sitting or lying down only, its hold ends by itself at three minutes, and it starts only after you confirm the cautions.

Relaxation and sleep. In a randomised comparison, progressive muscle relaxation increased felt relaxation and gave a steady physiological relaxation trend, while deep breathing briefly raised arousal first (Toussaint et al., 2021). Relaxation training is a conditionally recommended treatment for chronic insomnia (Edinger et al., 2021). Twenty minutes of slow breathing at six a minute before bed shortened the time to fall asleep and reduced night waking in people with self-reported insomnia (Tsai et al., 2015). The cognitive shuffle has only a conference study behind it so far (Beaudoin et al., 2016), and 5-4-3-2-1 grounding is a clinical tool with no direct trials.

Autogenic training. The Berlin psychiatrist Johannes Heinrich Schultz published autogenic training in 1932, and with Wolfgang Luthe made it a standard method: six exercises, each a short phrase said silently while you notice the body (heaviness, warmth, a calm heartbeat, the breath, a warm solar plexus and a cool forehead), learned one a week, practised two or three times a day, and ended with arms firm, a deep breath and eyes open (Schultz & Luthe, 1959). A meta-analysis of 60 controlled studies, 35 of them randomised, found medium-sized effects over control conditions, for tension headache and migraine, mild to moderate high blood pressure, anxiety disorders, mild to moderate depression and sleep problems among others, and results about as good as other psychological treatments, or a little smaller (Stetter & Kupper, 2002). A 2019 meta-analysis found it lowered anxiety in healthy adults and raised high-frequency heart-rate variability (Seo & Kim, 2019). At NASA's Ames Research Center, Patricia Cowings combined the exercises with biofeedback of heart rate, breathing, blood flow and skin conductance as Autogenic-Feedback Training, six hours of training that begins with paced breathing, against space motion sickness. On Spacelab 3 in 1985, the trained astronaut who learned to control his responses best had no severe symptom episodes, while the untrained crew members, who took medication, had several (Cowings et al., 1988); in a 2017 ground study, two hours of it before each test reduced the motion sickness of a simulated Orion re-entry (Cowings et al., 2017). Only a handful of astronauts have been studied. Entrain's version starts with paced breathing, as NASA's does, and a heart sensor gives the feedback.

Anxious or excited. Autogenic Reframing turns the method to anxious moments. Anxiety and excitement are nearly identical in the body: a faster heart, quicker breathing and a rush of adrenaline. In Brooks's experiments, heart rate rose before singing or a maths test whichever way people read it, and saying “I am excited” instead of trying to calm down made them feel more excited and do better (Brooks, 2014). Across randomised trials, reappraising stress arousal improved performance by a small amount (d = 0.22), more for public performances than written tests (Bosshard & Gomez, 2024). Nearly identical isn't quite the same: reading arousal as useful also shifted the cardiovascular response towards the more efficient pattern of a challenge rather than a threat (Jamieson et al., 2012). So the practice reframes the arousal rather than fighting it, while long out-breaths and the autogenic phrases take the edge off. It uses autogenic training's middle stage, where a personal resolution is said like one of the formulas, for “I am excited”, and rewrites the heart phrase to welcome a strong heartbeat as readiness. The combination hasn't been tested as a whole.

Why combine them? Each programme pairs techniques whose evidence points at the same goal at the same moment. Sleep Descent joins a delta-bound beat with slow pre-sleep breathing. Calm Reset pairs alpha-to-theta beats with cyclic sighs and resonance breathing. Focus Primer puts a beta prime after brief heart-rate-variability breathing, which improved reaction times and accuracy on a stressful Stroop task (Prinsloo et al., 2011). Exercise Recovery joins theta beats with slow breathing after training (McConnell et al., 2014; Sugimoto et al., 2025). Anapana reminders can be added to any programme: every few minutes, a quiet prompt brings attention back to the breath at the nostrils, the anchor of breath-awareness practice. No study has tested these combinations as packages, and a large 2023 trial found four weeks of coherent breathing didn't beat a placebo pace on self-reported stress (Fincham, Strauss & Cavanagh, 2023). A heart sensor is the honest check: each session ends with your own heart-rate change and coherence, so you can see what works for you.

Stretching and mobility. In a randomised trial with 134 workers at a logistics company, ten minutes of stretching after work for three months lowered anxiety compared with no programme, a moderate effect (partial η² = 0.06), and eased bodily pain and exhaustion (Montero-Marín et al., 2013). The Morning and Evening Stretch routines use the same kind of simple, everyday stretches, but haven't been tested themselves.

Capoeira. A 2025 systematic review found 19 studies of capoeira and health. Benefits for mental and social outcomes, such as attention, self-confidence, resilience and less aggression, were clearer than for physical health, where results for fitness, balance and bone density were inconclusive. Robust trials are still lacking, and most of the studies come from Portuguese-speaking countries (Köhler et al., 2025). Solo Capoeira teaches the basics on your own, to a synthesised berimbau playing the Angola rhythm.

Tapping. Emotional Freedom Techniques (EFT) pair tapping on acupressure points with attention on the problem and a self-acceptance statement. A review by the method's leading researchers counted 56 randomised trials, for conditions from anxiety and depression to pain and insomnia (Church et al., 2022). An independent 2025 review of seven trials in anxiety found reductions against no treatment and results similar to breathing or muscle relaxation, but couldn't pool the trials and judged most at some risk of bias (Choi et al., 2025). Whether the tapping itself matters is the weak point: in the three studies that could be pooled, real points did better than sham or no tapping by a moderate, borderline-significant margin (d = −0.47, 95% CI −0.94 to 0.0; Church et al., 2018). The nine points here are the standard ones: the side of the hand, then the top of the head, eyebrow, side of the eye, under the eye, under the nose, chin, collarbone and under the arm.

Naps. After a short night, a 10-minute afternoon nap improved alertness and performance straight away, with some gains lasting about two and a half hours. A 20-minute nap helped from about half an hour after waking, and a 30-minute nap brought a spell of grogginess first (Brooks & Lack, 2006). Heart rate drops at each transition from waking into light sleep and rises again with brief arousals (Burgess et al., 1999), which is what the Power Nap's sleep estimate relies on. It can't see sleep stages, so treat it as a guide.

Heart-led beats. This programme is an experiment that turns your heart data into sound, the idea behind biofeedback: as your heart rate settles below your baseline and its variability and coherence rise, the binaural beat glides from alpha towards theta. No study has tested binaural beats driven by the heart, and the rule is Entrain's own, so the chart at the end, your heart rate with the beat drawn over it, is the honest test.

Nonviolent communication. Marshall Rosenberg's method separates four things that usually blur together: what happened, what you feel, what you need, and what you'd like to ask for (Rosenberg, 2015). Self-Empathy and Have-Tos into Choices turn it inward. Judgments, labels and “shoulds” are read as signs of needs that aren't being met, and as rules you may have absorbed without choosing them, so questioning one becomes a small, personal kind of ideology critique. Research on NVC itself is early: in a randomised study with 312 medical students, two and a half days of training raised self-rated empathy three months later, though not other measures of empathy (Epinat-Duclos et al., 2021), and a 2025 review of 15 mostly uncontrolled studies found gains in empathy alongside weak methods (Pereira & Durgante, 2025). The parts the practices lean on are better studied. Putting feelings into words dampens their intensity (Torre & Lieberman, 2018); people who tell their emotions apart more precisely are less likely to cope in harmful ways when distressed (Kashdan et al., 2015), which is what the feelings wheel is for: 88 words in 11 families, to get from “bad” to “discouraged” or “resentful”; writing about difficult experiences has a small but reliable benefit across 146 randomised studies (Frattaroli, 2006); and in self-determination theory, acting from your own values goes with more well-being than acting from pressure, guilt or the need for approval (Ryan & Deci, 2000). What you write stays on your device, and you can keep nothing at all. The closing line paraphrases Carl Rogers's observation in On Becoming a Person (1961) that accepting yourself as you are is what makes change possible.

Difficult conversations. Four practices cover a hard conversation or a stressful meeting: Prepare a Hard Conversation (NVC) to get clear first, Difficult Conversation to get your body ready, During the Call beside a video call, and After the Conversation. The preparation asks what makes the conversation hard for you, including what it might say about you if it went badly, which the Harvard Negotiation Project calls the identity conversation (Stone, Patton & Heen, 1999), then your feelings and needs, theirs, a request and a first sentence. Writing about your worries for ten minutes just before an exam improved anxious students' results (Ramirez & Beilock, 2011). Getting ready starts in the body. Slow breathing at your resonance pace is the core of heart-rate-variability biofeedback, which reduced self-reported stress and anxiety with a large effect across 24 studies (g = 0.83; Goessl et al., 2017). The out-breath is the longer part here because at six breaths a minute a longer exhale felt more relaxing (Van Diest et al., 2014), though it didn't raise heart-rate variability more than even breaths did (Meehan & Shaffer, 2024). The beat plays before the conversation, where the meta-analysis found clearer effects than with listening only during a task; it settles into theta, the band with the larger effects on anxiety, then climbs back to alpha so you go in awake (Garcia-Argibay et al., 2019); that shape is Entrain's own. Four short steps come from experiments on performing under social pressure. Saying “I'm excited” rather than trying to calm down made speeches more persuasive and singing and maths more accurate (Brooks, 2014), and reading a racing heart as your body getting ready gave a more efficient cardiovascular response during a speech task (Jamieson et al., 2012). Reflecting on a value of your own beforehand blunted the cortisol rise to a stressful speech (Creswell et al., 2005). If-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals across 94 tests (d = 0.65; Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006), and talking to yourself by name or as “you”, as the plan is worded here, led to better speeches with less distress and less brooding afterwards (Kross et al., 2014). During the call, slow breathing at a moment of anxiety in daily life lowered it from just before to just after, though across weeks the effect on anxiety was mixed (Lachowicz et al., 2026); the beat plays at half volume on a low carrier so voices stay clear. Afterwards, a brief self-compassion exercise right after a speech reduced socially anxious students' brooding about it the next day (Blackie & Kocovski, 2018), and naming the feeling dampens it (Torre & Lieberman, 2018). None of the four has been tested as a whole.

Background sound and quotes. Summer rain, crickets and the nature sounds are synthesised on your device, like the pink-noise bed, and are there for comfort: masking sounds didn't weaken binaural beat effects in the research above. The quotes come from public-domain translations (George Long's Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus, Richard Gummere's Seneca, Max Müller's Dhammapada and James Legge's Tao Te Ching) and Thoreau's Walden. Carl Rogers's line is a paraphrase, since his book is still in copyright, and the lines signed Entrain are its own.

Your voice: chanting, laughing, letting it out and singing

Om chanting. The evidence for sounds you make yourself is thinner than for breathing, and for chanting it is small and early. In a pilot brain scan of 12 healthy volunteers, chanting Om aloud quietened the amygdala, the hippocampus, the anterior cingulate and other parts of the limbic system compared with rest, while saying “ssss” changed nothing there; the authors likened the pattern to vagus nerve stimulation and linked it to the vibration felt around the ears (Kalyani et al., 2011). A 2022 review linked Om chanting with calm, with delta and theta brain waves and with activity in the frontal lobes, and most of its studies reported less anxiety and stress, but their designs allow no firm conclusions yet (Thanneeru et al., 2022). The closing Mmm is closed-mouth humming, which raised nasal nitric oxide about 15-fold (Weitzberg & Lundberg, 2002), and any sound made on the breath out lengthens it. Om Chanting goes from aloud to softer to silent repetition, then silence, an order taken from practice rather than from a trial.

Laughter on purpose. Laughter you start on purpose, without a joke, as in laughter yoga, is the closest studied cousin of the playful sounds here. A 2019 review and meta-analysis found this simulated laughter more effective than laughter at something funny, and laughter-inducing therapies improved depression, though the studies were of low quality and their results varied a lot (van der Wal & Kok, 2019). Across randomised trials in people with physical or mental health problems, laughter-inducing interventions helped depression, anxiety, mood, stress and pain, anxiety most (Stiwi & Rosendahl, 2022). Laughing hard can leave you breathless, with a raised heart rate and a dry mouth, so the Laughter Break keeps its bouts short, with slow breaths between, and asks your mood before and after, so you can see what it does for you.

Letting it out. Making a sound when it hurts seems to help. In a cold-water test, saying “ow” let people keep a hand in the water longer than staying silent, though pressing a button worked about as well (Swee & Schirmer, 2015). Repeating a swear word at a steady, normal volume raised pain threshold and tolerance by about a third, while two made-up words, “fouch” and “twizpipe”, were found funnier and more emotional than a neutral word but left pain unchanged (Stephens & Robertson, 2020). Swearing also raised power on an exercise bike and grip strength (Stephens, Spierer & Katehis, 2018), and a shout as people squeezed raised their strongest grip in a study of 19 adults (Takarada & Nozaki, 2021). The researchers proposed that swearing works by letting go of holding back; in their later test, humour explained more of the effect, a finding they call exploratory (Stephens et al., 2023). Let It Out uses a steady, clear voice every three seconds, as the swearing studies did, with long breaths out after each round. Its Roar it out choice, a lion's breath, a wolf's howl and an owl's hoots, is play: no study has tested what imitating animals does to mood or brain waves, so it borrows from the laughter and vocal-release research, and from long breaths out, and claims nothing of its own.

Singing together. In a community choir, 90 minutes of singing raised closeness, positive mood and pain thresholds, a stand-in for the release of endorphins, by a similar amount in small groups and in a choir of 232 (Weinstein et al., 2016). In new adult education classes followed for seven months, singers felt close to each other sooner than people in craft or creative-writing classes, who caught up later: the ice-breaker effect (Pearce et al., 2015). Moving in time together and endorphins may both be at work, in music as in laughter, dance and sport done together (Tarr et al., 2014). Sing Together uses two traditional rounds, so everyone sings at once and each group still has a part of its own; on your own, the phone takes the other part. No study has tested singing along with a phone.

Nature sounds

Natural sounds such as birdsong, wind and water are often, though not always, heard as pleasant and restful: songbirds more than the calls of gulls or crows, some animal sounds not at all, and people differ in which ones they like (Ratcliffe, 2021). In an experiment with 95 students, listening to birdsong raised heart-rate variability, a sign of less physiological stress, compared with traffic noise (Ford et al., 2026). The guide behind Entrain's new techniques rates nature soundscapes such as birdsong and rain as moderate evidence, and advises letting people choose, since some find certain sounds unpleasant. So the background sound is a mix you make: rain or crickets, with birdsong, a stream, wind in the leaves or a crackling fire added on top, each on or off by itself, under any practice, breathing session or track. You'll find it in Settings, on Breathe and under a practice's Options.

A fire is the one sound with a study of its own. In three experiments with 226 adults, watching a fire with its crackle lowered blood pressure, and more the longer people watched, while the same fire with the sound off had no consistent effect (Lynn, 2014). That fire was seen as well as heard, so the crackle on its own hasn't been tested: here it's one more background sound, suited to the evening.

Each is synthesised on your device, with no recordings. The birds are whistles that glide, warble and trill in the patterns of a robin, a blackbird, a great tit, a chiffchaff and a wren, with a wood pigeon cooing further off; the stream is noise in two bands with thousands of small bubbles whose pitch rises as they ring; the wind brightens and swells in gusts, with the rustle of leaves rising with it; and the fire is a low roar, a soft hiss and crackles in clusters, with pops and sparks on a loop of another length. The loops differ in length, so together they don't repeat for a long time.

Sound and sleep

Sound can deepen sleep, but only when it's timed. Short bursts of pink noise played in step with the slow waves of deep sleep, which an EEG watches for all night, raised slow-wave activity and next-morning word recall in older adults (Papalambros et al., 2017), and in 14 volunteers, bursts timed the same way also strengthened the flow of the fluid that clears waste from the brain (Levitt et al., 2026). The memory effect is less sure than the early studies suggested: across 14 studies with 206 participants it shrank year by year, and the memory test used was barely reliable from one night to the next (Harlow et al., 2023). Why Entrain can't do the timing is under what it leaves out.

Noise played all night is a different thing. In a study of 72 students, those who slept with pink noise playing all night did no better at spotting a hidden rule in a puzzle than those who stayed awake, while those who slept in silence did a little better; the noise shortened the lightest stage of sleep, which went with insight. The authors suggest that overnight pink noise may cost some of what sleep does for insight and pattern detection, though few people found the rule, so it's far from settled (Vickrey & Lerner, 2023). The guide's advice is to avoid all-night noise as a default, and to offer timed or fading sound instead.

That's what Sleep sounds does: pink noise, rain, crickets, a stream or a fire that fades out over 15, 30, 45 or 60 minutes and then ends by itself, even with the player set to open-ended. A fade like this hasn't been tested on its own; it keeps the sound to the time it takes to fall asleep. And when a track is left open-ended late at night with noise or a background sound under it, the player says so.

Expectation, placebo and side effects

Recent breathwork trials compare a real technique with a convincing fake that people expect just as much from. In 400 adults, about ten minutes a day of coherent breathing at about 5.5 breaths a minute for four weeks improved stress, anxiety, depression and wellbeing, but no more than a placebo pace of 12 breaths a minute that participants found just as credible (Fincham et al., 2023). In the largest blinded trial of its kind, 200 adults did 20 minutes a day of fast breathing with breath holds, in the style of Wim Hof, or a placebo version, for three weeks; both groups expected the same, and stress improved in both by the same amount (Fincham et al., 2024). So much of breathwork's benefit seems to come from the practice itself (daily time out, relaxing the body, attention on the breath, and expectation) rather than from the exact pattern. Both trials were in healthy adults rather than patients, and in a month-long trial cyclic sighing still lifted mood more than mindfulness meditation (Balban et al., 2023).

Expectations shape results. In the Wim Hof immune study, men who expected more of the training reported fewer flu-like symptoms, and the more optimistic had more adrenaline and more of the anti-inflammatory signal IL-10 (van Middendorp et al., 2016). Expectancy, arguably the main part of the placebo effect, changes the effects of many treatments, mind-body practices among them. That's why each technique here states its evidence as it stands, and why the end screen sets your own numbers against your usual: honest expectations, and a way to see what works for you.

Side effects. Meditation can have them. Across 83 studies with 6,703 participants, 8.3% had an adverse event: 3.7% in experimental studies and 33.2% in observational ones. The most common were anxiety, depression and cognitive anomalies, and they could happen to people with no history of mental health problems (Farias et al., 2020). If a practice brings up strong feelings, open your eyes, look around the room and breathe slowly, and talk to someone you trust or a professional if it keeps happening; the safety notes say who should check with a clinician first.

How often and how long

Spreading practice across the day seems to work better than long single blocks, and how often you practise predicts benefit better than how long each session lasts. No study was found that compares back-to-back sessions with spaced ones directly.

  • Resonance breathing. The original protocol for heart-rate-variability biofeedback prescribed 20 minutes twice a day, with a warning to breathe naturally rather than overbreathe (Lehrer et al., 2000). Across studies since, people usually practise for 5 to 20 minutes once or twice a day at their own resonance rate (Lalanza et al., 2023). One practice guide suggests keeping home practice to 20 minutes a day, for a risk that is only theoretical; that is a minority, precautionary view.
  • Meditation. In a 2025 study that followed meditators over time, how often they practised predicted wellbeing, mood and distress better than how long they sat, and change worth noticing came with more daily practice than is usually advised: about 35 to 65 minutes a day for wellbeing and 50 to 80 for mental health (Bowles & Van Dam, 2025). The standard eight-week mindfulness courses ask for about 45 minutes of practice at home six days a week, and people do about two thirds of it on average (Parsons et al., 2017). In about 280,000 sessions in a meditation app, session length made no overall difference, while practising consistently, four to seven days a week, went with the largest improvement (Cearns & Clark, 2023).
  • In practice. Gentle practices (slow breathing, meditation, a body scan) can add up to longer daily totals when they're spread over separate sessions, such as a morning, a midday and an evening one. Intense techniques, such as Wim Hof-style breathing, belong in short sessions rather than long back-to-back blocks, because of the risk of fainting.

Combining techniques. Studies rarely test whether effects add up when techniques are stacked. In a pilot trial with 60 healthy adults, resonance breathing guided by live music raised well-being and heart-rate variability more than listening to recorded relaxation music (Fuchs et al., 2018). Entrain's programmes put together techniques whose evidence points the same way, as Guided practices explains, but the combinations themselves haven't been tested, so your own numbers after each session are the best guide.

What Entrain leaves out, and why

  • Pink noise timed to deep sleep. Short bursts of sound timed to the slow waves of deep sleep raised slow-wave activity, and with it next-morning word recall, in older adults (Papalambros et al., 2017). It works only closed-loop: an EEG has to watch for each slow wave all night and play the sound at the right moment. A phone can't see your brain waves, and a web page can't keep that kind of timing all night with the screen locked, so this stays research for now.
  • Smart bulbs. A web page can't reach the bulbs in your home: they answer their makers' apps, or a hub on your home network that a secure web page isn't allowed to talk to. Set them yourself instead: bright and cool in the morning, dim and warm in the evening, very dim and warm or red before sleep.
  • EMDR. Eye movements while recalling a memory tax working memory, and in laboratory studies they made distressing memories less vivid and less upsetting (Jeffries & Davis, 2013). But EMDR is a therapy for trauma, given by trained clinicians, so Entrain has no self-guided version: trauma work belongs with a clinician.
  • Candle gazing and panoramic vision. No good-quality studies of trataka or panoramic vision were found, so Entrain makes no claims for them.
  • Skin temperature, skin conductance and health apps. Rings and watches keep skin temperature and skin conductance to their own apps, and Apple Health and Google Health Connect open only to apps installed on the phone, not to a web page. Entrain runs in your browser and sends nothing anywhere, so it reads what a page can: a Bluetooth heart sensor and the microphone.

Safety

Entrain is for everyday stress, sleep and focus. It isn't a medical device or a treatment: nothing here diagnoses, treats or prevents any condition, and its heart readings are for practice, not for checking your health. Talk to a clinician about problems with sleep, anxiety or attention.

  • The beats. Ask your doctor first if you have epilepsy or another seizure disorder: the beats are meant to nudge the brain's rhythms, and their safety for people with epilepsy hasn't been established. Never listen while driving, cycling on the road or operating machinery, since the slower beats are made to relax you or send you to sleep. Keep the volume low.
  • Headphones with a pacemaker. The magnets in headphones and earbuds can interfere with a pacemaker or an implanted defibrillator. Keep them at least 15 cm (6 in) from it: not around your neck or in a breast pocket (American Heart Association).
  • Breathing. Breathe gently, and stop to breathe normally if you feel light-headed, dizzy or tingly. Leave out the breath holds and the fast breathing (Wim Hof, Kapalabhati, Morning Wake-Up's breath of fire, the power breaths before sport) if you are pregnant or have a heart, lung or blood-pressure condition or epilepsy, and never do the fast breathing in or near water, in the bath, standing up or while driving. Rest for an hour between blocks of fast breathing or long holds: back to back, they make fainting more likely, so Restart and Do it again ask first, and Wim Hof stops at eight rounds a day.
  • Breath holds. Swim & dive's long holds are done entirely at your own risk. A breath hold can make you black out without warning, so practise them only on dry land, sitting or lying down, never in or near water, in the bath or while driving. Breathe normally before each hold, never fast and deep to stretch it, breathe whenever you need to, and keep the second inhale a gentle top-up rather than forcing in extra air. In the water, train breath holds only with a coach or a trained buddy watching you.
  • Moving. Go only as far as is comfortable, stop anything that hurts, and keep clear space around you.
  • Feelings. A body scan, tapping or a reflection can bring up strong feelings. If one does, stop, look around the room and breathe slowly, and talk to someone you trust or a professional if it keeps happening. While a practice or the breathing guide runs, I need to stop ends it in one tap, fades the sound and opens Settle: slow breaths out with your eyes open, and the 5-4-3-2-1 grounding.
  • Food and supplements. Supplements aren't checked like medicines, and some interact with them: ask a doctor or pharmacist before you start one if you take medicines (blood thinners above all), are pregnant or breastfeeding, have kidney or liver disease, or have an operation coming up. The amounts in Healthy living are those used in studies, not prescriptions.
  • Light. Ask a doctor before you use a light box if you have bipolar disorder or an eye condition, or take medicines that make you sensitive to light. Shield your eyes from red and near-infrared devices, and use a light box, never a tanning lamp, for light therapy.

Check with a clinician before you start if you have a heart, lung or seizure condition, are pregnant, or are in treatment for anxiety, trauma or depression.
