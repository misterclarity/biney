    How it works, and what helps

    Each heartbeat sends a little more blood into your fingertip, which then lets a little less light through, so the camera sees the picture darken a shade about once a second. It's the way a pulse oximeter works, called photoplethysmography. The page averages the colour of 30 pictures a second, keeps the rhythm between 42 and 210 beats a minute, finds the darkest moment of each beat to within a few thousandths of a second, and passes the time between beats on to the same numbers a chest strap feeds: heart rate, the swing within each breath, RMSSD and coherence, and the resonance test and the Stress Check too.

    What helps: cover the lens and the light with the pad of one finger and rest it there lightly, since pressing hard squeezes the blood out and flattens the pulse. Keep your hand still on your lap or a table, and keep quiet: moving and talking swamp the pulse, which is what the signal meter shows, and beats count only while it reads fair or good. Warm hands give a stronger pulse than cold ones. Where the browser can't switch the light on, use a lamp or a window: daylight through the fingertip works too. The light can get warm; lift your finger for a moment if it does.

    For resting only. In studies, a fingertip on a phone's camera agreed closely with an ECG at rest, for heart rate and for RMSSD, and during slow breathing for biofeedback. Entrain's own way of reading it hasn't been tested against an ECG, or across skin tones, so it's marked experimental. The research, in Learn.

    Nothing is recorded or sent: each picture becomes three colour averages on this device and is gone. The camera stays on until you press Stop the camera, connect a sensor or close the page, and turns itself off after two minutes without a fingertip.

      What these numbers mean
      Heart rate
      Beats a minute. With a sensor that sends the time between beats, an ear clip or a chest strap, it's worked out beat by beat, so you can watch it rise as you breathe in and fall as you breathe out. A watch sends a rate it has averaged over a few seconds, so it moves more slowly.
      Swing
      How far your heart rate rises and falls within one breath: its highest minus its lowest. Breathing in speeds the heart and breathing out slows it, a rhythm called respiratory sinus arrhythmia. The swing grows as you breathe more slowly and evenly, and is usually largest near your resonance pace, about 4.5 to 6.5 breaths a minute. Last breath is the newest; this session averages them. Breaths count during a breathing session, on Breathe or in a practice, or whenever Breath from the microphone hears them.
      RMSSD
      How much each gap between heartbeats differs from the one before, in milliseconds (the root mean square of successive differences). It's the main short-term measure of the variability your vagus nerve, the brake of your rest-and-digest system, puts into your heartbeat: higher generally means more relaxed and recovered. It varies a lot between people and with age, so compare it with your own readings, taken the same way at the same time of day, rather than with anyone else's. Studies usually record five minutes; here it's the last 60 seconds, which needs beat-to-beat data.
      Coherence
      How much of your heart rhythm's variation falls into one smooth wave: the strongest peak between 0.04 and 0.26 Hz, against everything else, scored as HeartMath publishes it, usually from 0 to 8. Slow, even breathing raises it most reliably, and so does a warm feeling such as appreciation. It updates every 5 seconds from the last 64 seconds of heartbeats, so it takes up to a minute to show a change. The spectrum below shows what it measures.

      Breath by breathEach bar is one breath's heart-rate swing, in bpm

      Connect a sensor, then start a breathing session or turn on Breath from the microphone. Each breath adds a bar; taller, steadier bars mean you are breathing closer to your resonance rate.

      What this shows

      Each bar is one breath: how far your heart rate rose and fell within it, the newest brightest on the right. Bars come during a breathing session on Breathe or in a practice, timed by the guide; with Breath from the microphone on, every breath it hears adds one too, at any time.

      Taller bars mean your heart is following your breath more strongly, and bars of an even height mean a steady rhythm. They usually grow as you slow down towards about six breaths a minute, and shrink when breathing is fast, shallow or uneven, or when you move or talk. A watch smooths its heart rate over a few seconds, so its bars come out shorter than a chest strap's or an ear clip's.

      Resonance testConnect a sensor first

      Breathe at 6.5, 6, 5.5, 5 and 4.5 breaths a minute for two minutes each, and see which pace gives the biggest heart-rate swing. About 10 minutes, sitting still.

      What this shows

      Your resonance pace is the breathing rate at which your heart rate swings the most. Each breath's swing then falls in step with your blood-pressure reflex, the baroreflex, which has its own rhythm of about 10 seconds, so the two add up. In adults it usually lies between 4.5 and 6.5 breaths a minute, and breathing there is the core of heart rate variability biofeedback.

      The test follows the steps of the published protocol, without its rests between paces: two minutes at each of five paces, slowing from 6.5 to 4.5, then the average swing at each, leaving out the first breath at each pace while your heart settles. Each bar is one pace; the tallest is your result. Sit still, breathe gently through your nose rather than deeply, and keep the sensor still. Differences under about 1 bpm are within the noise, so between two close paces pick the one that feels easier.

      Heart rhythm spectrumPink: your heart rhythm · dashed: your breathing

      After 64 seconds of heart data, this shows which rhythms your heart rate is moving in. Coherence is high when one smooth peak stands out, usually right on your breathing pace.

      How to read it

      The chart splits the last 64 seconds of your heart rate into its rhythms, the way a prism splits light into colours. Left to right is how fast each rhythm is, in hertz, cycles a second; the height is how strongly your heart rate moves in it. Hertz times 60 gives breaths a minute: 0.1 Hz is 6 a minute, 0.2 Hz is 12.

      The shaded band, 0.04 to 0.26 Hz, is where the coherence score looks for its peak, and the darker fill under the peak is the 0.030 Hz window it counts as the peak. One tall, narrow peak means a coherent rhythm; low, scattered bumps mean your heart rate wanders with no rhythm leading.

      The dashed line is your breathing: during a session, the guide's pace; with Breath from the microphone on, the pace it hears. Your breathing usually makes the biggest peak, so when the two line up, your breathing is setting your heart's rhythm. A peak near 0.1 Hz with your breathing elsewhere comes from the blood-pressure reflex, whose rhythm is about 10 seconds. Researchers name two bands: 0.04 to 0.15 Hz, low frequency, mostly that reflex at rest and slow breathing below about 8.5 a minute; and 0.15 to 0.4 Hz, high frequency, the rhythm of breathing at ordinary paces.

      Turn it on and breathe as you normally would. The sound of your breath shows here as it rises and falls, with your heart rate under it when a sensor is on.

      How it works, and what helps

      Breathing is a soft rushing sound. The page keeps the part of the microphone's sound where breath noise lies, 0.5 to 5 kHz, above the beat's tones and a room's hum, measures how loud it is ten times a second, and finds the rhythm in that, the method a published study tested with a headset microphone under the nose. It needs about 25 seconds to find your pace, then updates every second, from the last minute. When in-breath and out-breath sound alike, the pace could read double; the guide's pace or your heart's rhythm settles which it is.

      What helps: the microphone close to your nose or mouth, the phone propped up 10 to 20 cm from your face, or wired earphones' microphone clipped near your chin. Gentle breathing through the nose is very quiet; a slightly longer, audible out-breath, through the nose or pursed lips, is far easier to hear. Slow breathing at about six a minute is the hardest pace to read, so give it time. Listen to any sound through headphones, since the beat or rain from a speaker drowns your breathing; talking and music get in the way too.

      Bluetooth earbuds and headsets switch to their call mode while their own microphone is in use, which plays sound in mono at call quality, and a binaural beat needs a different tone in each ear. So the page listens through the phone's or computer's own microphone, and through a Bluetooth one only if you choose it and no beat is playing; if a beat starts, it moves to the phone's own. The first time, before the browser names its microphones, the earbuds may switch for a moment while the page finds the phone's. Wired earphones with a microphone keep the beat in stereo too.

      The coherence score doesn't use it: it stays HeartMath's, from your heartbeat alone, so it stays comparable with their published figures. The microphone adds what your heartbeat can't tell: your actual breathing pace, to set beside your heart's rhythm, and when each breath happens, for the swing of every breath.

      Nothing is recorded or sent: the sound becomes one loudness number every tenth of a second on this device, and only the last two minutes of those are kept, for the chart. The microphone stays on until you press Stop listening or close the page.

      How it works, and what helps

      Each second, the page takes the last two seconds from each sensor, splits them into their rhythms and works out alpha's share (8 to 12 Hz) and theta's (4 to 8 Hz) of everything from 1 to 30 Hz, then averages the sensors with a clean signal. A share rather than a size, so how well each sensor touches the skin matters less. The trend sets the last 25 seconds against your first minute of clean signal: a change of more than 15% either way reads as rising or falling, a smaller one as steady. Start the trend again from now makes the next minute the one to compare with.

      Blinks, a frown, a tense jaw and movement all show up in a headband's signal, often far bigger than the brain's. So a stretch is left out when a sensor's signal swings more than 150 µV from top to bottom, as a blink or a movement makes it, or carries more than 10 µV of the fast 30 to 45 Hz buzz a tense jaw makes, and a sensor swinging more than 500 µV, or flat, is off the skin. A second counts when at least two sensors are clean. What helps: push your hair aside so the sensors behind the ears touch skin, sit the forehead sensors flat just above the eyebrows, dampen them a little if your skin is dry, and keep your jaw soft and your head still.

      Eyes open, eyes closed is a one-minute check: half a minute with your eyes resting on one point, then half a minute with them closed, between bells. Closing the eyes raises alpha within seconds, one of the most reliable effects in EEG (a typical day), so a clear rise shows the headband is reading you, and how big a rise is yours: Brain-Led Beats then counts a rise that size as a full one. Alpha usually rises as you relax with your eyes closed, and theta as you drift towards sleep or sink into meditation, but brain waves follow what the brain is doing: a rise is a picture, not a score.

      How it works

      Indoors, CO₂ builds up from everyone breathing in the room, so it shows how stale the air is. Outdoor air holds about 420 ppm. In a controlled office study, ventilation of 20 cubic feet a minute per person, with CO₂ above about 900 ppm, went with significantly lower scores on a test of decision-making, so above 900 ppm the focus pages say to open a window, and below it that the air is fresh enough. A reading counts for two hours; then the pages go back to the plain reminder.

      The Aranet4 measures every one to ten minutes, as it's set, and Entrain reads its latest measurement each time it has a new one, with the temperature and humidity it gives too. The first time, your phone or computer may ask for the six-digit code shown on the monitor's screen. Any other monitor: type the number it shows, and it's kept the same way. Nothing is sent anywhere: the reading stays on this device, and isn't in backups, since it belongs to this room.

      How it works, and which oximeters

      An oximeter shines red and infrared light through your fingertip and works out how much of your blood's haemoglobin carries oxygen, the SpO₂; at rest it usually reads 95 to 100%. During a practice's breath hold (Wim Hof Breathing's), or a hold in Breathe's pattern (Swim & dive's, or any pattern of your own with a hold), a reading below 90% ends the hold at once: the practice pauses with “Breathe now”, and Breathe ends the session. If the next step is another hold, it's left out too, and a hold that starts while the reading is still low ends straight away.

      Why only sooner: a fingertip reading trails the blood by several seconds, so by the time it shows a drop, your oxygen may already be a little lower. A wrist or ring reading is less accurate again. So the number is a rough safety signal, never a reason to hold on: breathe at the first strong urge, whatever it says. With no reading, or a finger out of the oximeter, holds run as they would without one. Entrain's own rule, from general knowledge rather than a trial.

      Entrain reads oximeters that use Bluetooth's standard pulse oximeter service (PLX); many low-cost fingertip oximeters use their own instead and won't connect. Nothing is recorded or sent: only the latest reading is kept, for this visit.
