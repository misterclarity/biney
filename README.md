# Entrain

Paced breathing, binaural beats and live heart-rate biofeedback in one offline web app, from a heart sensor or the phone alone, with brain trends from a Muse EEG headband. It covers guided practices and programmes, a stress check that advises what to do, yoga and capoeira routines, tapping, your voice (Om chanting, laughter, letting it out and singing rounds), music made on the device and a Music Break, scenes to watch, a fireside and restoration breaks, a screen light that follows the time of day, touch, guided imagery and yoga nidra, a cold finish to your shower, a monitored power nap, programmes for a mindful meal, waking up, sport, creativity and mindful sex, nonviolent-communication reflection, and Healthy living's recipes, supplements, routines, light, cold and heat, with one search box across all of it and Learn.

There are five tabs: Today, Library, Breathe, Heart and Learn.

- **Today** has your favourites, what you did lately and the last seven days, from one history of everything you do, with the heart-rate change and coherence whenever a sensor was connected. It also has a suggestion for the time of day and the six goals, and, until it's answered or put off with Not now, the offer of a short health check. History, behind it, charts the last twelve weeks or months: minutes, the heart-rate change from start to end, and coherence.
- **Library** holds every practice, breathing pattern and track, each under one goal (Sleep, Calm, Focus, Energy, Recover or Reflect), with your own routines and breathing patterns first. The filters combine: one goal or several, one technique or several, and length, posture and needs, again one or several each. Within a row the choices add up, the rows narrow each other, each chip counts what it would show, and a line under them says what's chosen, with Clear all. Each track has a page of its own, like a practice, and so do the custom primer and the beat builder.
- **Breathe** is the breathing guide, with six presets: Resonance 5.5, Cyclic sigh, Long exhale, Box 4-4-4-4, 4-7-8 and Swim & dive. Timings of your own can be saved under a name (up to twelve): each becomes a chip beside the presets, a row in the library, a search result and a star for Today, and can be updated, renamed or deleted, with an undo. Its visual is a circle, a wave, a box, a dial, a column or a scene that moves with your breath. With Phone on your belly (one of the new techniques: its switch carries the cup, and turning it on opens the donation dialog by name, then asks for motion access in the same tap where the browser asks, as Safari on an iPhone does), you lie back with the phone flat on your belly: each breath tilts it a little, and the page averages the motion sensor's readings into ten a second, band-passes them to 3 to 42 breaths a minute, takes the one direction they move in most, and finds the strongest rhythm in the last 45 seconds, so beside the guide it shows your breathing rate and whether you're with the guide, faster or slower (within 0.8 a minute, or 12%, counts as with it), says it in words under the guide with what to change, counts each breath, and after the session says how many it counted and for what share of the time it could read you, you were with the guide. It says when it's still counting (about 25 seconds), when the phone isn't moving with your breath or you're moving too much, when motion access wasn't given and when no readings come at all, as on most computers; its rate also becomes the spectrum's breathing line on Heart.
- **Heart** puts first what you need: connecting, the connection guide and the resonance test (with your last result and its pace) until a sensor is on; then your live numbers, the breath-by-breath chart, the test, the spectrum and breath from the microphone, with the guide at the bottom. Each number and chart has its explanation folded under it: what heart rate, the swing, RMSSD and coherence mean, what the bars show, what the resonance test finds and why, and how to read the spectrum (hertz into breaths a minute, the band coherence searches, the peak's window, the low- and high-frequency bands). The spectrum's dashed line is your breathing: the guide's pace during a session, or the pace the microphone hears, and a line under the chart says where your heart's rhythm peaks, your breathing pace and whether they match, or how to add your breathing when there's none. Breath from the microphone is an option: with echo cancelling, noise suppression and automatic gain off, it keeps the band where breath noise lies (0.5 to 5 kHz), measures its loudness ten times a second and finds the rhythm in the last minute (Nam et al. 2016's method), taking half that rhythm when in- and out-breath sound different and letting the guide's pace or the heart's own peak settle it when the sound alone can't. It shows a live trace of the breath sound with your heart rate under it, your pace (also beside the guide on Breathe), the swing of every breath it hears without a breathing session, how sure it is or what to change, the phone's or computer's own microphone by default, since Bluetooth earbuds switch to a mono call mode while their microphone is in use (a Bluetooth one only when you choose it and no beat plays; when a beat starts it moves to the phone's own, or stops if there's none; a choice of microphone, kept), and what went wrong when the microphone is refused, missing or busy. The coherence score doesn't use it and stays HeartMath's, from the heartbeat alone. Nothing is recorded or sent: only the loudness of the last two minutes is kept, on the device, and the microphone stays on until Stop listening or the page closes. Coherence and the spectrum need the time between heartbeats, so a sensor that sends heart rate only, such as a Garmin watch, gets neither: a watch averages its heart rate over a few seconds, which flattens the rise and fall within each breath. With one, the resonance test says it leans towards slower paces, for the same reason, and so does a result it gave. Without a sensor, Use the phone's camera (one of the new techniques, marked experimental, with the cup while the device is locked) reads your pulse from a fingertip at rest: the back camera with its light switched on where the browser allows it, each picture shrunk to a few dozen pixels and averaged, red (or green when red is clipped at full brightness) resampled to 30 a second, band-passed to 0.7 to 3.5 Hz without delay, the rate taken from the spectrum (checking with the wave's own repeats that a slow heart isn't read at a harmonic), and each beat's darkest moment found and refined between samples; a beat counts only after the signal has been good for a second and a half, when it looks like the window's other beats and its gap is near the rate and the gap before. The time between beats goes into the same pipeline a chest strap's does, so heart rate, the swing, RMSSD, coherence, the resonance test, the Stress Check and the end screen all work with it, and Breathe and the top bar show it. A meter shows the signal (no fingertip, settling, poor, fair or good), a live wave marks each beat counted, and the page says what to do: cover the lens and light lightly, keep still, find a lamp or daylight where the light can't be switched on. Frames come with the camera's own time stamps where the browser gives them, and from a timer otherwise, so it keeps reading while you're on Breathe; nothing is recorded or sent, and the camera turns itself off after two minutes without a fingertip. Connecting a Bluetooth sensor takes over from it. The connection guide opens on the HeartMath sensor; its Garmin steps start with a device that isn't paired with the watch, since the watch answers nothing from the phone it syncs with in Garmin Connect. On an iPhone or iPad, where no browser offers Bluetooth to web pages, the page points to Bluefy, a browser built for it. Connecting can't get stuck: linking up, finding the heart-rate service and starting the readings each have a deadline (half a minute for the last two on the first try, time enough to confirm a pairing request on the watch or the phone, which the message suggests after a few seconds), and a step that fails or runs out of time is tried again, three tries in all. The later tries give the sensor a few seconds to settle once linked (6, then 12) and check that it answers a test read before asking for its readings, and a wait that worked is kept for that sensor's next first try. The button reads Cancel meanwhile, and the message says which step it's on, or which didn't finish and what to try (such as a pairing request to accept or, for a Garmin watch, Garmin Connect holding on to it), with a folded list of what happened on each try, word for word, and a button to copy it when asking for help. After the heart's boxes, Brain trends from a headband (one of the new techniques, marked experimental: its button carries the cup and opens the donation dialog by name while the device is locked) connects a Muse EEG headband (the Muse 2, the Muse S or the 2016 Muse) over Bluetooth, as the open-source muse-js library does: the Muse service (0xfe8d), its control and four EEG readings (behind each ear and on each side of the forehead), then halt, preset p21, status and go, with the battery shown when the headband sends it. Each second, the last two seconds from each sensor are split into their rhythms (a Hann window, the power in each half-hertz from 1 to 45 Hz), and alpha's share (8 to 12 Hz) and theta's (4 to 8 Hz) of everything from 1 to 30 Hz is taken and averaged over the sensors with a clean signal: a stretch is left out when it swings more than 150 µV from top to bottom (a blink, a frown, a movement) or carries more than 10 µV of the 30 to 45 Hz buzz of a tense jaw, and a sensor swinging more than 500 µV, or flat, is off the skin, so each sensor shows good, noisy or off the skin, and the page says what to change. What it shows is a trend, never a state, as the guide advises for a consumer headband: alpha and theta rising, steady or falling, the last 25 seconds against your first minute of clean signal (more than 15% either way), with a chart of the 25-second averages and the first minute's levels dashed, and Start the trend again from now. Eyes open, eyes closed is its calibration: half a minute with the eyes resting on one point, a bell and the words to close them, half a minute closed, two bells to open them, then alpha's rise (the first five seconds of each half left out), what it tells about the fit, kept for Brain-Led Beats. While a practice runs, its chips show the alpha and theta trends against the practice's first minute (or say the signal is noisy), and its end screen sets the first minute against the last two, with a chart, an explanation and the change kept in the history. Connecting has deadlines for each step and Cancel; a dropped link reconnects by itself, up to three tries; the chooser closed, another device, a headband that sends nothing and one that doesn't answer each get their own message. There's no Web Bluetooth on an iPhone or iPad, so there the button is off and the page says where it works. Nothing is recorded or sent.

Techniques at a glance is the guide's table of techniques by sense (movement, breath, smell, sound, voice, vision, touch, taste, temperature, mind and feedback), with the state each aims at and its evidence as the guide rates it, each row linking to where Entrain has it or saying Learn only; a link to one of the new techniques carries the donation cup while the device is locked, and search finds every row. Then how a beat is made, monaural beats and isochronic tones (which need no headphones, with the evidence as the guide rates it: mixed, like binaural beats'), and what the beats, breathing, the heart sensor, breath training and each guided practice rest on; your voice (Om chanting, laughter on purpose, letting it out with "ow", a shout or a swear word, and singing together, with animal sounds as untested play); nature sounds (birdsong, wind and water often heard as restful but not by everyone, and a fire whose study watched it with its sound); scenes to watch (nature scenes raising alpha, on video too, fractals of mid complexity and fractal fluency, mostly from one lab, fresh roses, with flowers on a screen untested, and the fire, whose crackle it needs); nature breaks (Attention Restoration Theory's soft fascination, Berman and colleagues' walks in a park and through a city, and the reviews that found some kinds of attention helped and not others); sound and sleep (bursts of noise timed to deep sleep, which need an EEG, against noise played all night); music (lowering stress, most clearly heart rate, across two meta-analyses, more with a music therapist; slow tempo raising frontal theta and alpha and fast tempo beta and gamma; music you like doing more than music you don't); touch (slow stroking and the skin's C-tactile nerves, a hand on the heart or a hug in the self-soothing touch trial, weighted blankets, and massage and alpha, with the limits of each); temperature (cold water's rise in alertness chemicals and mood, cold shock and its rules, one trial of whole-body heating for depression, the limited evidence for regular saunas and a warm bath before bed); the mind (hypnosis and guided imagery, with the anxiety research and its limits, and yoga nidra's stages and trials); expectation and placebo, and the side effects of meditation; how often and how long to practise, and combining techniques; what to measure, and what a web page can and can't read; measuring with the phone alone (a fingertip on the camera against an ECG for heart rate, RMSSD and biofeedback, skin tone, a phone's accelerometer for breathing, and stillness, with Entrain's own readings marked untested); what Entrain leaves out and why (pink noise timed to deep sleep, smart bulbs, self-guided EMDR, gaze techniques, skin signals and health apps); the safety note; and the references, every citation with its source. All of it is free to read.
- **Settings** opens with the health check, then holds the defaults; options can also be kept for each practice. It also decides how the screen behaves: by default it stays on and at full brightness through every session. Under Your data it saves a backup of everything as one file and restores one, for a new phone or for safekeeping. Its Feedback form (also reached at #feedback, from search and from the impressum) takes an idea, a problem, praise or a question, with a title and, if you like, the app's build and your browser, and opens it as a new issue on the project's GitHub page, ready to send there: the app itself sends nothing, and the draft stays on the device until it's cleared. Scenes holds the scenes you find restful, with a picture of each. Screen light chooses the light of the screen during sessions, and asks whether you're a morning person, an evening person or in between. New techniques says whether they're unlocked on this device, with its device code and what the donation opens. About shows the build, a short id made from the sources.

The Stress Check, first under Calm, asks how stressed you feel from 0 to 10, gives you 45 seconds to settle, then reads your heart at rest for two minutes, or five: your resting heart rate and, from a sensor that sends the time between beats, such as the HeartMath ear clip or a chest strap, RMSSD, leaving out pairs of beats that jump more than 25%, with how many beats it has and whether the signal is steady shown while it measures. No sound plays, so nothing nudges the reading, and beats that arrive while it's paused are left out. Your heart is set against your own usual, never anyone else's: the middle of your last ten full checks taken at rest. The first three set that baseline; from the fourth, RMSSD below 90%, 75% or 55% of your usual, or a resting heart rate more than 5 or 10 bpm above it, raises the level. A check within half an hour of another session shows what that session changed and stays out of the baseline, and one that ends early or whose signal is too unsteady adds no RMSSD to it. The level is the higher of your heart's and your rating's, and the end screen says that the rule is Entrain's own, not a validated stress scale. The advice comes first on the end screen, before the chart: a practice for the level, with its binaural beat and its breathing read from the practice's own plan, so it says what will play at your resonance pace or the pace you set (Heart Coherence under its alpha beat for a low level and its theta beat for a moderate one; Calm Reset, cyclic sighs then resonance breathing, under its alpha-to-theta slide for a high level and a steady theta beat for a very high one), why, naming the studies, an alternative (Sleep Descent late in the evening), the track and breathing pattern to do it without a programme, and a button that opens the practice with that beat and length chosen. A check within three hours of the last one shows first what changed since: RMSSD, resting heart rate and felt stress, and what you did in between. Without a sensor, or with one that sends heart rate only, the level follows your rating and whatever the sensor can give. Each check goes into the history with its RMSSD and your rating.

Heart Coherence is three minutes of heart-focused breathing, then breathing with gratitude: the feeling comes second because it's easiest to feel once slow breathing has settled the heart rhythm, as it is after a meditation. It can be done sitting, lying down or walking, for 10, 15 or 20 minutes, over a theta, alpha or alpha-to-theta beat or none, at its optimal default (your resonance rate: the test's answer, or 5.5 a minute until you take it) or at an in and out of your own. Those choices are kept for it, like its other options.

Gratitude is a practice of its own: three or four minutes of slow, even breathing at the same pace choices first, because gratitude is easiest to feel once you are calm, then gratitude for something small from today, for a person who helped you and for your own body, a minute of savouring the feeling, and an invitation to write down three good things. It runs 10 or 15 minutes, sitting, lying down or walking. Calm Reset has the same beat and pace choices as Heart Coherence: its usual alpha-to-theta slide, a steady theta or alpha beat, or none, and the optimal default pace or an in and out of your own for its resonance breathing (the cyclic sighs keep their rhythm), kept for it.

For a hard conversation or a stressful meeting there are four practices. Difficult Conversation (6, 10 or 15 minutes) settles the body first: cyclic sighs, then slow breathing at your resonance rate with a longer out-breath (40% in, 60% out), with live coherence from a sensor and a beat that slides from 10 Hz alpha into 6 Hz theta for the breathing and climbs back to alpha for the last steps, so you go in calm and awake. Four short steps follow: reading a racing heart as readiness, a value of your own, your first sentence, and an if-then plan said to yourself by name. It can begin with an NVC practice, chosen on its page under Begin with: Prepare a Hard Conversation (NVC), written for it, which asks what happened, what makes the conversation hard for you, your feelings and needs, a guess at theirs and a request, then drafts a first sentence from your answers for you to make your own; or Self-Empathy. That practice's end screen leads straight on, and Difficult Conversation brings back the needs, request and first sentence, from the same session or from a preparation kept in the last twelve hours. During the Call runs beside a video call until you tap The call's over: a 10 Hz beat (16 Hz for long meetings) at half your usual volume on a low 200 Hz carrier, a silent pacer to glance at while you listen, or a reminder every five minutes, and your heart rate and coherence. It starts silent, with no voice, bells, breath tones or vibration, and the call itself stays silent even with the voice on. After the Conversation lets the stress settle: sighs and long out-breaths under a theta beat, naming the feeling, kindness to yourself, what went well and one next step. These defaults are the practices' own, set over the ones for every practice and changeable like any option; Anapana reminders start off in all three. Each end screen suggests the one that comes next.

Autogenic Training is Schultz's standard method, the one NASA adapted for astronauts as Autogenic-Feedback Training. A short spell of paced breathing with a longer out-breath comes first, as in NASA's version. Then six exercises, each a short phrase said silently while the voice says it softly with you and the body map lights the part it names: heaviness and warmth limb by limb, a calm and regular heartbeat, the breath that breathes itself, a warm solar plexus and a cool forehead, with "I am completely calm" after each, then all of them together, and back with arms firm, a deep breath and eyes open. Each phrase comes back every twenty seconds or so, to repeat silently in between. Its choices: lying down, an armchair or the coachman's seat; all six, or the week of the classic course you're in (each week adds one exercise and gives it the most time); the heartbeat phrase, a warm chest, or neither; and back to alert, or into sleep. It runs 5 to 20 minutes, with a binaural beat if you add one and a heart sensor as the feedback. Autogenic Reframing turns it to anxious moments: naming what your body is doing, the reminder that anxiety and excitement are nearly identical in the body, cyclic sighs and long out-breaths, short autogenic phrases with the heart phrase rewritten to welcome a strong heartbeat as readiness, then "I am excited" (or "I'm ready for this", or "This energy helps me") to answer the anxious thought, said again as its intention formula. Each suggests the other at its end. A practice that ends in sleep (Autogenic Training's bedtime ending, Sleep Descent, the Cognitive Shuffle) ends without the end bells.

Mindful Meal is for eating a meal slowly and attentively, at breakfast, lunch or dinner, in 20, 30 or 45 minutes. Before the first bite: a moment to arrive, your hunger rated from 0 to 10, a minute and a half of slow breathing at your resonance pace with a longer out-breath (or a minute of cyclic sighs), a moment of gratitude for the meal and the people who brought it to you, then the food looked at and smelt and the first two bites taken slowly, noticing taste, texture and temperature. The rest of the meal runs at your own pace with the screen dimmed, until you tap I've finished or the time you chose runs out, with a reminder to pause every two and a half minutes or so; halfway through, one asks about your hunger. Then your hunger again, set beside the first on the end screen and in the history, gratitude if you chose it for after, and a breath before you get up. Every part is a choice on its page, kept for it, with the usual one chosen for you: the hunger check, before and after or left out; slow breathing, cyclic sighs or straight to the meal, at the optimal default pace or your own; gratitude before, after, both or not at all; the guided first bites, or not; reminders spoken softly, as a soft chime with a few words on the screen (it sounds even with the bells off), or silence; and a steady 10 Hz alpha beat at half your usual volume, a 6 Hz theta beat, or none, for a meal with others. The other options are there as for any practice; Anapana reminders start off, and tapping isn't offered. The end screen suggests a short walk, since light walking after a meal lowers the rise in blood sugar. Each dish's recipe ends with a link to it, and so do breakfast, lunch and the evening meal in the routines.

Morning Wake-Up is Sleep Descent the other way round, for 10, 15 or 20 minutes on waking: a long stretch in bed (or sitting up), then breathing that starts slow, with the out-breath longer, evens out and quickens, the in-breath leading, while a binaural beat rises from 6 Hz theta through 10 Hz alpha to 16 Hz beta (or only to alpha); two rounds of breath of fire if you choose it; stretches in bed or seated and a few standing poses with drawings, or the breathing only; then daylight, a glass of water and one thing you'd like from today. It's Today's suggestion early in the morning, and the morning routine links to it.

Sports Performance is for cycling, running, rowing or weight training. Each sport brings its own settings, which you can change, and picking a sport again brings them back; the options that don't apply (the cadence tick for weights, the sets and rests for the others) stay out of the way. Before a session, in 5 or 10 minutes: slow breathing at your resonance pace (for weight training, power breaths and the bracing breath), a rehearsal of the session in your mind, and a few words of your own for when it gets hard, while the beat rises from 10 Hz alpha to 15 Hz beta (a steady 15 Hz for weight training). During, if you choose it: an endurance session runs until you tap Session over, at most two hours, with a cue every five minutes or so (technique, breathing and your words) and a soft tick at your cadence (on for running, at 170 steps a minute, or slower or quicker); weight training goes set by set, tapping Set done after each and resting with slow breathing for the time chosen. A cool-down follows, and the end screen suggests Exercise Recovery.

Swim & dive, on Breathe, is breath-hold training for swimmers and freedivers on dry land: a slow, full breath in, a gentle top-up, a hold, a long breath out and a hold with the lungs empty, starting at 5, 2, 15, 10 and 5 seconds. Its sliders go further than the other patterns', in whole seconds: up to 60 for the in-breath, both holds and the out-breath, and up to 30 for the top-up (the second inhale). Any other preset brings back the usual range, and patterns of your own made in it keep it. It is done entirely at your own risk, and says so in its settings and beside the start button. The first time it's opened (picked, reached from the library, search or Learn, or left chosen on Breathe), its seven cautions come up at once, and it can be used only after "I understand the risks"; "Not now" goes back to the pattern before. After that it starts like any other pattern, and the cautions can be read again from the stage: dry land only, sitting or lying down, never in or near water or while driving; normal breathing before each hold, since hyperventilating delays the urge to breathe while oxygen keeps falling; breathe whenever you need to; no lung packing; a doctor first with a heart, lung or blood-pressure condition, epilepsy, fainting or pregnancy; and in the water, a coach or a trained buddy watching. Learn's Breath training for swimming and diving sets out what the research says about lung size, the breathing muscles, breath-hold time and limited breathing in the pool, and why breath holds are dangerous in water.

Creative Flow, in 15, 20 or 30 minutes: slow breathing to ease in, open awareness, a game to loosen up (unusual uses for everyday things, or random words to link to your question), ideas for your own question, then a daydream, an easy walk or Edison's twilight (dozing with a spoon in your hand) to let them brew, and time to capture them; or straight on into the work until you tap I'm done. Its beat is 10 Hz alpha, dipping to 6 Hz theta while ideas brew.

Mindful Sex, in 20, 30 or 45 minutes, with a partner or on your own: arriving and, with a partner, agreeing what you'd love and that either of you can pause at any time; slow breathing together; soft eye contact; slow touch in turns (sensate focus); then lovemaking at your own pace with the screen dimmed and a reminder every four minutes or so to come back to breath and sensation (a soft chime, spoken softly, or silence) until you tap that you're done; and afterglow. Choose slow and present, with a longer out-breath under a 6 Hz theta beat, or building intensity, with fuller breaths under a 10 Hz alpha beat. Learn sets out what the research shows about that choice, and what it doesn't: no study has tested binaural beats during sex.

Four practices use your own voice, each a new technique. Om Chanting (5, 10 or 15 minutes) teaches the chant, then paces Om on every breath out, four seconds in and a long Aaa, Uuu and Mmm (8 to 15 seconds, your choice), the Mmm a hum with the lips closed: aloud, then softer, then in your mind, in whole breaths so each part starts on a breath in, and ends in silence; a soft, low drone on D, made on the device, can play while you chant aloud, and its breath cues are chimes. Laughter Break (5 or 10 minutes, on your own or with others) asks your mood from 0 to 10, then has you clap along to a ho, ho, ha ha ha tick, then laugh on purpose in short bouts (greeting, a giggle, milkshake, silent, an imaginary phone call and hearty laughter) with slow breaths between, ends with slow breathing, and asks your mood again, set beside the first on the end screen and in the history; its caution names breathlessness, a raised heart rate and a dry mouth, and asks you to skip it with a hernia, after recent surgery, with uncontrolled high blood pressure or late in pregnancy. Let It Out (3 or 5 minutes), before a workout or after a stressful moment, gives three or four rounds of your sound on a soft tick every three seconds, said steady and clear: "ow", a short shout, a word of your own (a swear word if you like) or the swearing study's made-up words "fouch" and "twizpipe", each round followed by long breaths out; Roar it out swaps them for a lion's breath, a wolf's howl and an owl's hoots on paced, forceful breaths out, offered as play that no study has tested. Sports Performance before weight training suggests it on its end screen. Sing Together (10 or 15 minutes), for people in one room or on your own, warms up with a hum over the drone and five notes on each vowel, then takes Frère Jacques and Row, Row, Row Your Boat (traditional rounds, free to sing) three ways: listened to, sung together twice through and sung as a round in two, three or four parts; the words are on the screen with the syllable to sing marked, a soft synthesised tune leads after a count-in at a steady tempo (or slower), and in a round each group has a row saying when it comes in and where it is. On your own, the phone sings the first part and you come in after it. Any time left goes to more rounds and a soft hum of the tune. Pausing, skipping a step and I need to stop silence the tune and the drone at once.

Two practices are about touch, each a new technique. Soothing Touch (5 or 10 minutes) paces slow strokes along your own forearm at about 3 cm a second, the speed the skin's C-tactile nerves respond to best: a drawing of the forearm with a dot that glides from the wrist to the elbow on each long breath out (4 seconds in and 8 out, a 24 cm stroke; or 3 and 6, 18 cm), lifting back to the wrist as you breathe in, first the left arm, then the right (twice each in ten minutes), ending with a hand on your heart. A soft sweeping tone, a whoosh rising over a tone a fifth below it, made on the device and scheduled on the audio clock, plays with each stroke, and under the arm a row of centimetre marks at real size moves at the real speed while you stroke. Real size starts from the screen: a CSS centimetre on a computer, the usual phone density on a touch screen (a phone shows a CSS centimetre at about six tenths of a real one), and Match a bank card, under its Options, makes it exact: a box a card's height (5.4 cm) sized to a card laid on the screen, kept on this device and left out of backups. Its other choices: a soft brush or velvet instead of your fingertips (an optional extra whose evidence is early), a self-hug (arms crossed, the same slow strokes up the upper arms, then a held hug) and a hand on the heart (a hand on your chest, then one on your belly, as most chose in the self-soothing touch trial, slow circles and rest, all on a longer breath out). Self-Massage (8 or 10 minutes) walks round the body map with moderate pressure: each hand and forearm, the jaw and temples, the scalp, the neck and shoulders and each foot, each area lit on the body map with its pressure and stroke under its name (thumb circles, a gentle squeeze, long strokes, kneading) and the seconds left on it; With a partner gives the hands and forearms, shoulders and neck and scalp to each of you in turn, swapping giver and receiver halfway, after agreeing how to say more or less. Its caution: leave out injuries, wounds, rashes, bruises and swelling, go lightly round varicose veins, keep the pressure light with blood thinners, leave a painful, swollen calf alone (it can be a clot), and keep away from the front of the throat. Both carry the health check's caution for touch, written for trauma, panic or dissociation. Sleep Descent's page and the healthy sleep habits routine suggest a weighted blanket where restlessness at night comes with anxiety or a low mood, free to read: low-risk, but not for small children or anyone who couldn't take it off unaided.

Two practices are for the mind, each a new technique. Guided Imagery (10, 15 or 20 minutes) follows the usual pattern of a hypnotic relaxation: your eyes rest on one spot while you breathe slowly with a longer breath out (4 seconds in, 6 out) until your eyelids grow heavy, then a chime in place of the step bell, sounding even with the bells off, asks you to close them; a slow count from ten down to one, each number said and drawn large on the screen, fading in; then the place you choose (a beach, a forest, a mountain lake or a place of your own) explored through each sense in turn, what you see, hear, feel and smell, and a while just to rest there, the orb on the screen in the place's colour (sand, green or blue); then the suggestion you choose: calm you can bring back later with three slow breaths out and a touch of thumb and finger, confidence for something ahead, or deep rest. Back to alert counts you up from one to five, your eyes opening on five; Into sleep lets the scene fade and leaves two minutes or so to drift off, ending with no bells or words. Yoga Nidra (20, 30 or 45 minutes) is yogic sleep, or non-sleep deep rest, in Swami Satyananda Saraswati's stages, lying down: listening to sounds far and near, a resolve (sankalpa) of your own or one of three offered, said three times; a quick rotation of awareness round the body, a point named every three seconds from the right hand thumb through the right side, the left, the back and the front to the whole body together (96 points; 47 in 20 minutes; in 45, round again a little faster, every two and a half seconds), each part lit on the body map with a dot where the point is; the natural breath counted backwards from 27; heaviness then lightness, warmth then coolness; quick images, one every five seconds or more; in the longer sessions a spell of quiet rest; the resolve again; and back to alert (the breath, the room, a slow stretch) or into sleep. Each can play a 6 Hz theta beat underneath if you add it, off to start with, which slides to 3 Hz delta through Into sleep and stays there. Their cautions: either can bring up memories or strong feelings, or leave you feeling detached or unreal, so with psychosis or a dissociative disorder, now or in the past, talk to your doctor or therapist first; never while driving; and for Yoga Nidra, lie on your side in later pregnancy. Guided Imagery carries the health check's imagery caution, Yoga Nidra both the imagery and the body ones.

Cold Finish, a new technique, ends your shower on cold, for morning energy. The phone sits outside the shower with the volume up, so everything that matters is loud. You start it as you step in: a warm shower for most of the 3, 5 or 8 minutes, a warning half a minute before the cold to finish washing, 18 seconds of slow breaths still in the warm (3 in, 6 out), then three strokes of a low bell, on a sound of its own well above the usual bells, to turn the water cold (or just cool the first few times). Through the cold, a voice at the start of each long breath out ("Cold now. Let the gasp pass, and breathe out, long and slow", halfway on the longer ones, "Nearly there" at the last), a low tone falling with each breath out (it can be left out), and on the screen the seconds left in large figures round a pacer that shrinks as you breathe out (still under reduced motion), then three pips and three strokes of a higher bell to turn the water off, and half a minute to dry off and warm up by moving. The cold lasts 15, 30, 45 or 60 seconds, or Build up chooses from your history: 15 seconds to begin with; after three at one length, the third at least a week after the first, 15 seconds longer, up to a minute; a session cut short in the cold doesn't count, and after a month away it steps back once. The end screen shows the seconds of cold and where your build-up stands, and the history keeps them. Its caution: cold shock (a gasp, fast breathing and a sudden rise in heart rate and blood pressure) with a heart condition, high blood pressure or pregnancy, starting gradually, never with Wim Hof breathing or breath holds, never alone in open water, and a steady footing. Its page asks for the health check first while it's unanswered, then shows the health check's cold caution written for you, and a tick box comes before Start; after fast breathing or long holds within the hour, it says to let the breathing settle first. The morning routine suggests it, and Morning Wake-Up's end screen suggests it after the Focus Primer. Healthy living's Cold and heat tab, free to read, has a card for cold water and plunges and one for saunas and hot baths.

Two practices come from the guide's advice on nature and fire, each a new technique, and Walking Meditation has a new way to do it. Fireside (20, 30 or 45 minutes, under Calm and good for sleep, best in the evening) is the fire from the scenes, with its crackle all the way through, since in the study a fire on a screen relaxed people only with its sound, and more the longer they watched: three quarters of a minute to settle, three minutes of slow breaths (4 seconds in, 6 out) as the flames rise and settle with them, unless you choose Just the fire, then just the fire, with a quiet word about every six minutes, and a minute to come back to the room. Restoration Break (3, 5 or 10 minutes, under Recover and good for focus) is for between blocks of work: one of the scenes you find restful (Yours, a different one each day, or one you pick) with the nature sound that suits it (wind for the clouds, the leaves and the fractal ridgelines, a stream for the water, birdsong for the forest and the flowers, the fire's crackle), or your own background sound instead; one opening line, then, from ten seconds in, no voice, step name, captions or words on the screen, only the scene, the controls and a faint time left, since content that takes effort to read works against the soft fascination that's meant to rest your attention. Its end screen has no quote and suggests taking the next break outside, leading to Walking Meditation, whose new Where choice, In a park, turns its guidance to the path and then to what catches your eye: clouds, leaves, birds, water and their sounds; In a park opens with the donation dialog until the device is unlocked, and a park kept from before reads as a quiet path meanwhile (a choice's options can be new techniques: `sup` lists them, and Settings' New techniques lists this one). Brain-Led Beats (15, 20 or 30 minutes, under Calm), a new technique marked experimental, tries the guide's EEG neurofeedback with sound, as Heart-Led Beats does with the heart: with a Muse headband connected on the Heart page, a minute to settle, then a minute and a half reading your resting alpha and theta with your eyes closed, then every five seconds a binaural beat moves towards a target set by how far your alpha (60%) and theta (40%), averaged over the last 25 seconds, have risen above that rest. A rise of 30%, or the rise your eyes-closed check found, is the full share; the beat runs from 10 Hz down to 6 Hz and back up if they fall, never faster than 0.15 Hz an update, and with no headband data it follows a scripted descent. The session's picture shows the beat over your alpha and theta, and the end screen the two trends, the beat and a chart of all three. Its evidence is stated as the guide gives it: neurofeedback moderate, mixed but promising, a consumer headband good for broad trends only, and the rule Entrain's own and untested.

Updates install themselves. The app looks for a new version each time it opens, when it comes back to the screen after half an hour away, and every hour it stays on screen; a new version downloads in the background. It then installs (the page reloads into it) at a moment that interrupts nothing: straight away if the app hasn't been touched since it opened, otherwise the next time you leave it, once no practice, breathing session, resonance test or track is under way, no heart sensor is connected, the microphone isn't listening for your breathing, the camera isn't reading your pulse, the phone isn't counting your breaths on your belly, no EEG headband is connected, nothing is being typed and no routine or recipe is half done. The player's clock ticks once a second. The app opens without building the library, which is made each time you open it; its practices' lengths are worked out while the phone is idle after start-up.

## Build

```
python3 src/build.py
```

This writes `index.html`, `sw.js` and `manifest.webmanifest` at the top level. It also writes `entrain-page.html`, a preview page without the offline helper, which is not committed. A framed page can't download files itself, so in that preview Save a backup goes through the viewer's `downloads` capability, which the artifact has to declare.

The build only assembles, nothing is rewritten on the way:

- **Markup.** Each `{include:name}` line in `src/html/app.html` is replaced by `src/html/name`, and `{i:name}` anywhere in the markup becomes an icon from `src/html/icons.svg`. The build stops on an icon that isn't in the sprite.
- **Script.** The files in `src/js/` are joined in the order listed in `build.py`, inside one function, so they share one scope. `core.js` comes first and `boot.js` last. Every other file only declares things; `boot.js` wires the page up in order. Because the scope is shared, the build stops if a top-level name is declared twice. Otherwise the second declaration would silently replace the first.

## Test

You need Node 18 or later, Playwright with Chromium (`npm install playwright`, then `npx playwright install chromium`) and Python 3.

```
node tests/run.js              # every suite, as many at a time as there are cores (up to four)
node tests/run.js --jobs 1     # one at a time, about twenty minutes (two at a time takes ten)
node tests/run.js apptest      # one or more suites by name
```

See `tests/README.md` for what each suite covers.

## Activation codes

The new techniques open with an activation code: the device code, a dot and an ECDSA P-256 signature of it, made with your private key. The page holds only the public keys, in `SUP_KEYS` at the top of `src/js/supporter.js`, and checks codes with the browser's own cryptography. `src/unlock.js` (Node 18 or later, no dependencies) signs them. A new feature joins the gate by carrying `sup: true` (a practice, a pattern in `BREATHS` or a track in `TRACKS`) and calling `supGate(name, go)` wherever it starts something of its own; the usual ways in (Start, Play, picking a pattern, the library, search, Today and the end screen) already check the flag.

- **Keep the private key outside the repository**: by default `~/.entrain/entrain-unlock-private.pem`, readable only by you, or wherever `ENTRAIN_UNLOCK_KEY` points (or `--key PATH`). Keep a copy somewhere safe: without it, no new codes can be signed. Git ignores `*.pem` files, apart from the tests' own key.
- **For each donation**, check on Ko-fi that it is €1 or more, then sign the device code from its message:
  ```
  node src/unlock.js sign ABCDE-FGHJK               # the activation code and its link, for one or more device codes
  node src/unlock.js sign --from ko-fi-email.txt    # every device code in an email or a CSV export, with the line it came from
  ```
  Send the donor the link (`https://misterclarity.github.io/biney/#unlock/…`, or another with `--base URL`). It unlocks only the device it was made for. `sign` refuses a key whose public half isn't in `SUP_KEYS`, since the app wouldn't accept what it signs.
- **To check a code** someone sends back: `node src/unlock.js verify <code or link>` says which device it unlocks and whether a trusted key signed it.
- **To change keys** (planned, or because the private key may have leaked): `node src/unlock.js keygen --add` makes a new private key (never over one that's there; `--out PATH` chooses where) and adds its public half to `SUP_KEYS`, after the old ones. Keep the old public keys there so the codes already sent keep working; take one out only if its private key leaked, and know that the codes it signed stop working too. Then rebuild, commit and publish, and sign with the new key.
- The suites sign with a key of their own, made when they first run and kept in `tests/out/` (which git ignores), so no private key is ever in the repository. It opens nothing in the real app: its public half is never in `SUP_KEYS`, and they trust it only in a copy of the page they write to `tests/out/`.

## Publishing

`.github/workflows/test-and-publish.yml` runs on every push to the default branch. It checks that the committed `index.html`, `sw.js` and manifest are what `src/build.py` makes from the source, runs every suite, and publishes the site to GitHub Pages only if they all pass. For that, the repository's Settings > Pages > Build and deployment > Source has to be **GitHub Actions**. While it is still "Deploy from a branch", GitHub publishes every push straight from the branch, tested or not, and the workflow only runs the tests and says so in its summary.
